## 1. Research question

To test whether the three-way split of conflict statements into **avoidance**,
**integrative** and **distributive** is valid, by checking whether the coded categories
relate, as predicted, to (a) how observers perceive the speaker and (b) the speaker's
nonverbal behaviour (abstract).

In other words, this is the **validation study for a coding scheme**, not a study of who uses
which tactic. The scheme itself had been developed two years earlier in a study of roommate
conflict (via Song 2015, p. 17).

## 2. The categories / scheme

**The unit of coding is the statement.** Each statement made during a conflict is assigned
to one of three classes (abstract). This is what distinguishes it from Rahim's five styles
(04), which describe a *person's general tendency* from a questionnaire.

| Class | What it covers (via Song 2015, pp. 17–18, paraphrasing this paper's p. 83) | Doorway example *(my illustration, not from the paper)* |
|---|---|---|
| **Distributive** | verbally competitive or individualistic behaviour — pressing one's own position at the other's expense | "I'm going first, you'll have to wait." |
| **Integrative** | verbally cooperative behaviour; statements aimed at a resolution that works for both sides | "I'm taking someone to the ER. Where are you headed? Can you step back for a moment?" |
| **Avoidance** | statements that deny there is a conflict, shift the conversation away from it, or deal with it only indirectly or ambiguously — minimising explicit discussion | "Oh, no problem, whatever." / changing the subject |

**Subcategories.** The full scheme has finer tactics nested inside the three classes, defined
in the 1986 manual. **I could not open any source that lists them**, so they are not
recorded here as findings.

**How the scheme developed later** (via Song 2015, pp. 18–19):
- Sillars and colleagues later re-organised conflict tactics along two dimensions:
  **directness** and **cooperation** (Canary, Lakey & Sillars 2013). In that version,
  confrontative remarks count as direct-competitive, while analytic and conciliatory remarks
  count as direct-cooperative.
- Sillars & Canary (2013) added a further category, **polysemic** strategies: acts that can
  fit more than one category because they can be read in more than one way. The motivating
  problem is that information sharing, arguments and concessions can serve either
  cooperative or competitive ends depending on context.

## 3. Empirical basis

- **Two studies** (abstract).
- They examined how coded verbal tactics related to **observer perceptions** of the speaker's
  disclosiveness and competitiveness, and to predicted **nonverbal behaviours** (abstract).
- **Participants, setting, number of conversations or statements coded:** `[not accessible]`.
- The scheme's origin data were **conflicts between college roommates** (via Song 2015,
  p. 17). Its later applications include marital conflict (reference list on the Cambridge
  Core page for the 2018 chapter).

## 5. Reliability

**`[not accessible]`.** No agreement-between-coders figure (e.g. Cohen's κ) appears in the

reliability figure very likely exists in the full text or the manual. **Obtaining it is the

What *is* available is evidence of **continued use**: Caughlin & Vangelisti (2006) describe
researchers "using Sillars' (1986) Verbal Tactics Coding Scheme or similar measures" to
separate integrative from distributive behaviour (issue #12 review, entry 7) — a sign the
scheme is established in the field, though not a reliability statistic.

## 6. Operationalisation — how a category gets assigned

- **Unit:** one statement (abstract).
- **Decision:** which of the three classes the statement belongs to, based on its function
  in the conflict — competing for one's own outcome, cooperating toward a joint one, or
  steering away from the conflict (via Song 2015, pp. 17–18).
- **Detailed decision rules:** in the 1986 manual — `[not accessible]`.

**A known difficulty, from the scheme's own later development:** the same statement can serve
different functions (via Song 2015, p. 19), which is why a "polysemic" category was later
added. [inference] This matters for the FYP: a statement such as "I'm taking a patient to
hospital" shares information (integrative) but also makes a claim to go first
(distributive). Any coding of LLM dialogue needs a rule for statements like this, or a
"polysemic" code.

## 7. Limitations stated by the authors

From the abstract, as recorded in the issue #12 review: much of the link between verbal
tactics and nonverbal behaviour in study 2 **came from how talkative integrative speakers
were**. In other words, the integrative category partly tracks the *amount* of talk, not
only its cooperativeness.

Other limitations: `[not accessible]`.

## 8. What else the scheme's literature says about outcomes

Via Song (2015, p. 20), summarising Sillars (1980a), the roommate study:
- integrative behaviour predicts **constructive conflict resolution**;
- avoidance is **negatively related** to conflict resolution.

Treat these as secondary reports of a 1980 study; they were not read in the original.

## 9. Relevance to this FYP

*[inference throughout, except where a locator is given]*

1. **It's the right *kind* of scheme for LLM dialogue.** Unlike Rahim's questionnaire (04),
   it was built to label **statements in a conflict conversation**, which is exactly what a
   GameChat-style transcript consists of.
2. **Three classes is enough to start, and it agrees with other frameworks.** Several style
   frameworks collapse Rahim's five into three by folding compromising into integrating and
   accommodating into avoiding (via Song 2015, p. 18, citing Donohue & Cai 2014). The three-way
   avoid / compete / cooperate split therefore has independent support.
3. **The talkativeness confound is a direct threat to LLM coding.** LLMs are wordy by default.
   If "integrative" partly measures how much is said (§7), LLM agents may be coded as more
   cooperative than they really are. Countermeasure: normalise by statement count, or code
   *function* strictly regardless of length.
4. **Worked example — coding a GameChat-style exchange at statement level:**

   | Turn | Statement | Code |
   |---|---|---|
   | A | "I'm taking someone to the hospital for an emergency." | integrative (shares information) — or polysemic, since it also claims priority |
   | B | "I'm going to the airport, my flight leaves soon." | integrative (shares information) |
   | B | "Yours is more urgent, go ahead." | integrative — a concession toward a joint outcome |

   *(Illustrative only, not taken from any paper.)* Note that GameChat's instruction to agents
   — no pleasantries, be concise (03, §IV-C) — pushes all statements toward the integrative
   class. A human sample would contain more avoidance and distributive statements, and that
   difference is itself something the FYP could measure.
5. **It says nothing about how a conflict ends.** It labels tactics, not endings, so it should
   be paired with Pütz & Hassan's termination formats (05).
