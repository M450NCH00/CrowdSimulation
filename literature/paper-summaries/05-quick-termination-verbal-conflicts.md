## 1. Research question

How team members **negotiate the ending** of a verbal conflict at work (Abstract).

The authors chose meetings where computer scientists discuss the progress of their
projects, describing this as a setting where resolving conflict is **crucial for work to
continue** (Abstract). That framing matters: participants can't simply walk away, so they
have a practical incentive to end disagreements quickly.

## 4. Findings

All from the abstract.

1. **Conflicts are short.** Most conflicts end after **two or three disagreements**.
2. **Frequency order of endings:** **submission** is the most frequent, followed by
   **stand-off**, then **compromise**.
3. **Participants actively keep conflicts short.** They minimise conflict by **ending it as
   soon as someone signals they are unwilling to concede**. *(This finding was present in the
   abstract but missing from the first version of this file.)*
4. **How they keep it short depends on how it ends.** The pattern of conflict minimisation
   varies with the termination format the participants negotiate.

**Counts, percentages, and the table they come from: `[not accessible]`.** Findings 1 and 2
are the only human base rates for conversational conflict endings found anywhere in this
repository's reviews, which is why the missing counts matter.

## 8. Relevance to this FYP — detailed

*[inference throughout, except where a locator is given]*

### 8.1 Why this is the closest human comparison for conversational conflict endings

- It studies **conflict carried out entirely in talk**, and it labels **how each episode
  ended** — the same question the FYP asks of an LLM dialogue (who gave way, or did anyone).
- Its participants have a **practical reason to finish quickly** (work must continue;
  Abstract). Two agents blocking a doorway have the same pressure: the conflict has to end
  for either to move.
- It is one of only two sources in the repository with **human base rates** for conflict
  outcomes in conversation. The other is KODIS, ~18% impasse in text-chat disputes (issue #12
  review, entry 10).

### 8.2 What it predicts LLM dialogue should look like, if it's human-like

| Human pattern (Abstract) | What to check in an LLM doorway dialogue |
|---|---|
| Most conflicts end after 2–3 disagreements | Count disagreements per episode. Do LLM agents end as fast, or talk longer? (GameChat caps each agent at four messages; 03, §IV-C.) |
| Submission is the most common ending | Does one agent give way in most episodes? |
| Stand-off is second most common | Do LLM agents **ever** reach a stand-off? GameChat reports agreement was always reached (03, §V-B.2) — which would be a clear departure from this human pattern. |
| Compromise is least common | In a doorway, a "compromise" might be both agents slowing or one stepping partly aside. How often does the LLM propose one? |
| Conflicts end as soon as someone signals they won't concede | Once one agent states it won't give way, does the other stop arguing — or keep negotiating? |

### 8.3 Direct links to points already raised with the supervisor

- **The early-yield idea** (talking points, §1.3): "if the other's task outranks mine, yield
  without stating my own". Humans already do something close to this — they **stop the
  conflict as soon as unwillingness to concede is signalled** (finding 3). That gives the
  idea a human precedent, not just an efficiency argument.
- **Over-cooperation** (talking points, §1.5): stand-off is the *second most common* human
  ending. A system where agreement is guaranteed cannot produce it at all.
- **A physical twist:** in a doorway a verbal stand-off can't last, because someone has to
  move eventually. In GameChat, a failure to agree hands the decision to the fallback rule
  (closer agent goes first; 03, §IV-D). So a human-like LLM system should occasionally end in
  a stand-off that the fallback then resolves, and the rate of that is measurable.

### 8.4 Proposed coding procedure for LLM doorway dialogues

1. **Mark the start of a conflict:** the first disagreement that is *continued* into a second
   turn (the paper's definition). Single-turn polite exchanges ("after you" / "thanks") aren't
   conflicts under this definition.
2. **Count episode length:** the number of disagreements until the episode ends.
3. **Code each statement** with Sillars' three classes (see
   [`12-sillars-verbal-tactics-coding.md`](12-sillars-verbal-tactics-coding.md)).
4. **Code the ending** as submission / stand-off / compromise — **using provisional
   definitions until the full text is obtained**:
   - *submission:* one agent explicitly gives way and the other proceeds;
   - *stand-off:* both keep their positions and the exchange ends without agreement (in
     GameChat terms, the fallback decides);
   - *compromise:* both adjust (e.g. both slow, or one partly yields in exchange for
     something).
5. **Compare distributions** against the human ordering (submission > stand-off >
   compromise) and the 2–3 disagreement length.

### 8.5 Worked examples *(illustrative, not from any paper)*

**Submission, 2 disagreements:**
> A: "I need to go through first, it's an emergency."
> B: "I'm in a hurry too — I'd rather go first." *(disagreement 1)*
> A: "Mine is a hospital run." *(disagreement 2)*
> B: "Alright, you go."

**Stand-off, ended by a signalled refusal (finding 3):**
> A: "Please let me through first."
> B: "I can't wait, I'm going now." *(disagreement 1)*
> A: "I'm not stepping back either." *(disagreement 2 — refusal signalled)*
> — exchange ends; the fallback rule decides.

**Compromise:**
> A: "I'm late, can I go first?"
> B: "I'm carrying something heavy." *(disagreement)*
> A: "Okay — you go, I'll take the side door after."

### 8.6 Caveats on transferring this paper

- **Workplace colleagues with power relations**, not strangers. Deference to a senior
  colleague may drive some "submissions" in a way no doorway encounter would reproduce.
- **Computer science project meetings** — a specific professional culture; location and
  language not stated in the abstract.
- **Termination-format definitions are unread.** Until the full text is obtained, any FYP
  coding with these labels uses **provisional** definitions (§8.4) and must say so.
- **The frequency ordering is a rank order only**; without counts, a statistical comparison
