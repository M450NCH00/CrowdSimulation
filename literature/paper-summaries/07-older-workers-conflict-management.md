## 1. Research question

Whether the two components of occupational future time perspective (OFTP) — focus on
opportunities and focus on limitations — account for age differences in which conflict
management strategies employees use at work (Abstract; Introduction).

The authors motivate this by noting that earlier studies disagree about the age pattern in
conflict responses, and speculating that the disagreement arises because the two OFTP
components pull in opposite directions (General Discussion, opening paragraph).

Two hypotheses are stated explicitly:

- **H1:** age will have a negative indirect effect on constructive strategies (integrating
  and compromising) through focus on opportunities (Introduction, H1).
- **H2:** age will have a positive indirect effect on passive strategies (obliging and
  avoiding), and a negative indirect effect on the destructive strategy (dominating), through
  focus on limitations (Introduction, H2).

## 2. Sample

**Study 1** (Method of Study 1, Participants):
- N = 416 Hong Kong Chinese workers.
- 57.7% female.
- Age range 20–68; M = 39.13, SD = 12.42 (also Table 1).
- 48.8% managerial employees and professionals; 51.2% other occupations (clerical,
  service-oriented or technical).
- 64.9% held a bachelor's degree or higher.
- Recruited through the affiliated university's alumni mailing list and flyers posted on
  campus; eligibility was full-time or part-time employment. Administered online via
  Qualtrics, roughly 15 minutes, compensated with a supermarket voucher worth HKD50
  (approximately US$6.5). Ethics approval H001009.

**Study 2** (Method of Study 2, Participants; Procedure):
- N = 268 Hong Kong Chinese managerial employees and professionals.
- 53.7% female.
- Age range 23–66; mean 42.56, SD = 9.48 (also Table 2).
- 75.4% held a bachelor's degree or above.
- Almost 90% worked as managerial or administrative employees; 11.2% were professionals.
- Recruited through organisations' human resources departments. Of 58 organisations that
  responded to the invitation, 27 agreed to take part, a response rate the paper gives as
  46.6%. Questionnaires were distributed by internal mail or email and returned directly to
  the researchers. Compensation was a supermarket voucher worth HKD100 (approximately US$13).

**Country and culture:** Hong Kong, described by the authors as a setting where collectivistic
virtues including power distance and respect for elders are observed at work (General
Discussion, Focus on Opportunities and on Limitations as Mediators).

## 3. Constructs and instrument

**Conflict strategies.** Five strategies from Rahim's dual-concern framework: integrating,
compromising, obliging, avoiding and dominating. The paper groups them into three functional
classes of its own (Introduction):

- **constructive** — integrating and compromising;
- **passive** — avoiding and obliging, described as bringing short-term benefit by limiting
  the escalation of negative emotion;
- **destructive** — dominating, described as involving expressed anger and confrontation to
  force the other party to give in.

The authors add that integrating and compromising are commonly seen as adaptive, while
obliging, avoiding and dominating are often regarded as maladaptive because of unfavourable
psychological or work-related outcomes (Introduction).

**Instrument.** The **Chinese version of the Rahim Organizational Conflict Inventory-II
(ROCI-II)**, 28 items covering the five strategies, each rated on a five-point Likert scale
from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), higher scores meaning greater use.
Permission to use the inventory was obtained from the Center for Advanced Studies in
Management (Study 1, Measures, Conflict Management Strategies).

A confirmatory factor analysis of the Chinese version is reported in the same section:
χ²(138) = 474.40, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.90, TLI = 0.87, RMSEA = 0.08, SRMR = 0.07.

Cronbach's alphas in Study 1 were 0.81 (integrating), 0.72 (compromising), 0.82 (obliging),
0.70 (avoiding) and 0.77 (dominating) (Study 1, Measures).

**OFTP.** The 10-item OFTP scale, five-point Likert. Cronbach's alphas were 0.88 (focus on
opportunities) and 0.80 (focus on limitations) in Study 1 (Study 1, Measures), and 0.80 and
0.68 respectively in Study 2 (Study 2, Measures, OFTP).

**⚠ Measurement type: self-report in both studies, not observed or transcribed talk.**
Study 1 asked participants to indicate their typical ways of handling disputes with
co-workers — habitual use (Study 1, Measures). Study 2 asked participants to recall a personal
conflict incident with a co-worker from the previous six months and report their use of the
five strategies in that incident (Study 2, Measures, Personal Conflict Incident in the
Workplace). **At no point were conflict conversations observed, recorded or transcribed.**

## 4. Design and analysis

Two cross-sectional questionnaire studies.

- Study 1 measured habitual strategy use; Study 2 measured reported strategy use in one
  recalled real-life incident. The authors give their reason for adding Study 2: retrospective
  recall of a specific incident should make contradictory combinations, such as simultaneous
  obliging and dominating, less likely (end of Study 1 discussion).
- **Analysis:** the PROCESS macro, Model 4, with focus on opportunities and focus on
  limitations entered **simultaneously as parallel mediators** (Study 1, Indirect Effect of
  Age…; Study 2, Indirect Effect of Age…).
- **Covariates differ between studies.** Study 1 controlled education and occupation, because
  both correlated significantly with the OFTP dimensions or the strategies. Study 2 controlled
  gender and education. (Same two sections.)
- **Gender was dropped as a covariate in Study 1** because it was not correlated with either
  OFTP dimension or with any of the five strategies (Study 1, Descriptive Analyses).
- In Study 2 the authors additionally checked a quadratic age term; see §5.
- Study 2 also verified that conflict type, the co-worker's gender, and the relationship with
  the co-worker (supervisor, peer, subordinate) did not differ between younger (39 or below)
  and older (40 or above) participants — χ²(2) = 1.45, p = 0.229 for conflict type;
  χ²(2) = 2.59, p = 0.270 for gender; χ²(2) = 5.35, p = 0.069 for relationship — and that none
  of the three was associated with the five strategies, so all three were excluded from the
  analyses (Study 2, Measures).
- An **alternative mediation model** was also tested, reversing mediators and outcomes; see
  §7.

### 5b. Direct effects

**Study 1 — reported qualitatively in the text, not numerically.** The General Discussion
states that among the five strategies, direct effects of age appeared **only for the habitual
use of obliging and avoiding**, and that the direction is that **younger employees tend to use
these two passive strategies more than older employees** (General Discussion, Focus on
Opportunities and on Limitations as Mediators, first paragraph; the numbers are in Figure 1).

The authors flag this as a **failure to replicate**: they note it does not reproduce the
age-related increase in passive strategies reported in earlier studies they cite (same
paragraph). Their proposed explanation is given in §6.

**Study 2 — `[not stated in paper]` in the text.** Figure 2 carries `c'` values but the body
text does not report which Study 2 direct effects were significant.

### 5d. Hypothesis outcomes as the authors state them

- **H1 supported** in both studies: older workers perceived fewer opportunities, which was in
  turn associated with less use of integrating and compromising (Study 1 and Study 2, Indirect
  Effect of Age…).
- **H2 partly supported.** The predicted positive indirect effects on obliging and avoiding
  through focus on limitations appeared. The predicted *negative* indirect effect on dominating
  did not — the observed effect was **positive**, contrary to prediction, in both studies
  (Study 1 and Study 2, Indirect Effect of Age…).

### 5e. Gender

Gender was **not** correlated with either OFTP dimension or with any of the five strategies in
Study 1, and was excluded from that study's mediation analyses on that basis (Study 1,
Descriptive Analyses). Table 1 gives the gender column (coded 1 = male, 2 = female) as 0.02
for integrating, 0.06 for compromising, 0.02 for obliging, 0.01 for avoiding and −0.08 for
dominating, none flagged as significant.

In Study 2, gender **was** correlated with age (−0.21, p < 0.001) and with focus on
opportunities (−0.14, p < 0.05), and was therefore retained as a covariate; its correlations
with the five strategies in Table 2 are −0.09, 0.02, 0.04, 0.03 and 0.01, none flagged as
significant.

**This paper reports no substantive gender difference in the use of any conflict strategy.**

## 6. Authors' explanation

The framework is **socioemotional selectivity theory (SST)** (General Discussion). The
mechanism the authors propose:

- People who focus on opportunities prioritise knowledge-related goals; people who focus on
  limitations prioritise emotional goals, which then shapes how they respond to conflict
  (Introduction).
- Older workers perceive fewer occupational opportunities, which reduces their use of
  constructive strategies, because those strategies are seen as paying off in long-run career
  development (General Discussion).
- Older workers who perceive greater limitations emphasise present-oriented goals, making
  emotion regulation more salient; rather than resolving the conflict they prefer avoiding or
  obliging to limit its emotional cost (General Discussion).
- A second explanation the authors offer for the same path is **work disengagement**: workers
  nearer retirement are more likely to disengage, and obliging and avoiding are ways of
  disengaging (General Discussion).
- For the **direct** effect — younger employees obliging and avoiding *more* — the authors
  offer a different, culturally specific explanation: younger workers have less work
  experience and are therefore more likely to go along with colleagues' judgements, and they
  suggest this is especially likely among Hong Kong Chinese employees because of workplace
  power distance and respect for elders (General Discussion).
- For the unexpected positive path to dominating, the authors speculate that older workers may
  value co-worker relationships less than close relationships such as family and friends
  (General Discussion).

## 7. Limitations stated by the authors

From the section headed Limitations and Future Studies:

1. **Closeness to the conflict partner was not measured.** Under SST, older adults prefer
   emotionally close partners, and the authors note older individuals may use passive
   strategies less with less close co-workers than with close ones.
2. **Both studies relied on self-report**, which they say may be subject to recall and social
   desirability biases. They raise older adults' positive memory bias as a specific risk, since
   it might lead older participants to report conflicts they handled satisfactorily. They
   recommend adding an objective measure, such as ratings from the opposing partner, and a
   social desirability scale.
3. **Cross-sectional data cannot establish causality** between OFTP and conflict management.
   They report an exploratory alternative model in which the five strategies were entered as
   mediators and the OFTP dimensions as outcomes; its pathways were not significant except the
   indirect effect of age on focus on limitations through avoiding (B = −0.002, SE = 0.001,
   95% CI [−0.004, −0.000]). They recommend experimentally manipulating perceived opportunities
   and limitations in future work.

## 8. Transferability to this FYP

`[inference]` — this section is my judgement, not the paper's.

**What does not transfer:**

- **Setting.** Workplace conflict between co-workers with an ongoing relationship, a shared
  employer and often a hierarchy (the study records whether the partner was a supervisor, peer
  or subordinate). The FYP's target is strangers meeting once for a few seconds in a public
  space with no relationship, no hierarchy and no future interaction.
- **Timescale and mechanism.** The paper's entire explanatory mechanism is *occupational future
  time perspective* — how much career runway a person perceives. That construct has no analogue
  in a doorway encounter. The mediator that carries almost all of this paper's age effects
  simply does not exist in the FYP's setting.
- **Measurement type.** Self-reported habitual or recalled strategy use, never observed talk.
  The FYP wants to validate generated *dialogue*; this paper contains none.
- **Population.** Working adults aged 20–68 (Study 1) and 23–66 (Study 2). It says nothing
  about children, adolescents or the elderly, three of the five bands in
  `dataset/personas.yaml`. Study 2 is additionally restricted to managerial employees and
  professionals.

**What may transfer:**

- **Culture.** Hong Kong Chinese is the closest cultural setting to Singapore among the five
  papers in this issue, and the authors explicitly invoke East Asian workplace norms.
- **The construct vocabulary.** Obliging and avoiding are the two Rahim strategies that most
  closely correspond to yielding, and this paper measures both with a validated Chinese
  instrument.
- **The warning about direction.** The paper demonstrates that an age effect can reverse sign
  depending on whether a mediator is modelled. That caution transfers even though the specific
  mediator does not.

## 9. Relevance to this FYP

`[inference]` — my judgement.

1. **It is the closest available human reference point for an age effect on conceding, and it
   does not deliver a simple one.** Anyone wanting to claim "older people yield more" cannot
   cite this paper for it. In Study 1 the *direct* effect runs the other way, in Study 2 there
   is no zero-order age effect at all, and the positive age→obliging/avoiding relationship
   exists only as an indirect path through perceived limitations.
2. **The null gender result is the more directly usable finding.** In a 416-person sample using
   the standard instrument, gender predicted none of the five strategies. Group L papers that
   report gender effects in LLM conflict behaviour have no support from this paper for the
   existence of such a difference in this population and setting.
3. **Which Group L paper could it be compared against?** Most plausibly
   `10-fairness-llm-negotiator.md`, which is the only Group L paper here where something is
   actually contested between two parties and where conceding is measurable. The comparison is
   weak and the mismatches are set out in `reading-notes-demographics.md`; it should not be
   made without them.
4. **A methodological idea worth stealing.** The paper's design separates a raw age effect from
   a mechanism-mediated one and shows they can point in opposite directions. If the FYP ever
   analyses LLM-generated yielding by age, reporting only a raw difference risks exactly the
   error this paper was written to correct.
