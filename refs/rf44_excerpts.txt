   25| Understanding multi-agent dynamics is one of the most pressing challenges in AI alignment. Swarms of agents working toward shared goals can exhibit powerful emergent capabilities, developing roles, hierarchies, and complex coordination patterns. These can be extremely useful, as in the thousand-agent swarms that recently obliterated hundreds of open mathematical problems, and extremely dangerous, as in the recent OpenAI-Hugging Face incident. Like biology, studying agent dynamics requires both formal models (to describe the range of possible behaviors) and empirical research (to see what behaviors arise in practice).
   26| 
   27| We teamed up with [link: https://olamlabs.ai/] Olam Labs, which builds multi-agent environments for frontier labs, to run a series of experiments in Minecraft and Mindustry.
   28| 
   29| Minecraft
   30| 
   31| Minecraft is a natural environment for studying swarm behavior. Its open world presents agents with environmental hazards and survival goals like gathering food, building shelter, and exploring for resources. It's also familiar, fun, and has a history as an AI testing ground, from OpenAI's [link: https://openai.com/index/vpt/] video pretraining work to Google DeepMind's agents [link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22319] defeating the Ender Dragon.
   32| 
   33| We wanted long-running agents that could play Minecraft. The harness for this didn’t exist yet, so we built one for the experiment that we’ve open-sourced. Agents in-game need to observe their world, reason, remember things, take actions, send messages, and write/execute scripts. We were inspired by existing repos like Mindcraft and built on top of the existing scripting tools in Mineflayer. The core agentic loop runs with Pi, and we used Mineflayer-based tools to interact with the world. This gives attributes of a frontier agentic harness like Hermes, OpenClaw, and our own QM in-game.

 1010| 
 1011| Bed Wars is a popular game format where teams fight to destroy each other’s bed while protecting their own. In our instance, teams started on separate islands, with currency spawners between them in no-man’s-land. Agents could build bridges to cross between islands and buy upgrades using currency collected in the center of the map.
 1012| 
 1013| A team wins by destroying the opposing side’s bed, then killing their remaining players. As long as a team’s bed is present, players respawn after dying. Once a team’s bed is destroyed, their players no longer respawn, meaning their next death eliminates them from the match. This may seem complicated, but think of the team’s main objective as defending their bed and destroying their opponent’s. That said, if no beds were destroyed in a given match, the team with the most kills won.
 1014| 
 1015| In almost every run, the GPT agents were much more aggressive and coordinated in their strategies to destroy the Claude team’s bed. This was an inherently riskier strategy vs. Claude’s more defensive posture that tried to win by kill count.

 1123| The setup
 1124| 
 1125|  [link: https://github.com/Anuken/Mindustry] Mindustry is an open source game by Anuken. Full credit to him for the amazing game and letting it be open source for cool experiments like this!
 1126| 
 1127| It’s a factory-building game with tower defense and real-time strategy: you mine resources, build conveyor supply chains and factories, and turn that economy into turrets and an army. In its PvP mode, which we used, two teams race to build the stronger economy and military and destroy the other team’s core.
 1128| 
 1129| This makes it a great environment for testing multi-agent dynamics. Mindustry is 2D, so it can be played easily by agents, is replayable, and is prone to adversarial pressures that can test these swarms under competitive social conditions that they may face in the real world.
 1130| 
 1131| We structured the simulated environment to have two teams of agents spawn on each side of a map, with the same resources on both sides. They play a best of N series, where you set how many games the teams play. When launching it, we set the composition of the teams (which harness + model, and how many), then let it run! The agents run in their own sandbox and the game is exposed to them through an API, so they are able to run scripts and interact with the game however they like in their own Linux environment.
 1132| 
 1133| Both teams have the exact same API to the game, without any special instructions given to them on how to play - neither team is given anything more than the other team.
 1134| 
 1135| Multi-agent swarms, like in [link: https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/67869394-cb91-4c12-888c-5cbd85c7814c/OpenAI-Hugging-Face%20Incident-Technical-Report.pdf] the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident, can turn simple adversarial conditions, like being in a sandbox with an unsolvable task, into a hive-minded ecology of agents that take over compute clusters, hack Hugging Face, and more. Studying how these swarms operate in reproducible game environments like Mindustry that mimic real-world scenarios can be both crucial (and fascinating) to understanding what these systems may look like in our future.

 1188| 
 1189|  [link: https://github.com/olamlabs/harbor-multiagent] Multi-Agent Harbor: the framework that runs them, our fork of [link: https://github.com/harbor-framework/harbor] Harbor, the open-source framework behind Terminal-Bench. Our fork runs multiple agents in any environment. We use it for a variety of things (enterprises, industrials, etc.), and it’s very useful for game-based multi-agent simulations like this one. For more on the framework, point your agent at it.
 1190| 
 1191| Multi-Agent Mindustry comes with two environments. One is a best of N series like this run, with teams composed however you like from generic coding-agent harnesses and models, for example 5 Claudes + 5 GPTs against 5 Geminis + 5 Groks in a best of 5. The other, Endless Survival, has one team of up to 20 agents hold off endless enemy waves.
 1192| 
 1193| We intentionally do no harness-level customization or special memory work. Each agent gets its own Linux environment, a generic harness and the game’s API, writes scripts and takes notes as it pleases, and relies on the harness’s native compaction (which is quite good). Nobody tells it how to play, so it’s free to specialize, play and (optionally) try whatever it wants. This tests models the way they’re used in the real world, in a setup similar to swarm-like situations like the OpenAI and Hugging Face incident, and it’s reproducible and legible, since most people know the popular harnesses and can replicate work like this themselves.

   33| 
   34| The five game tools are `query`, `events`, `act`, `say` and `wake`. Each is a `POST` to its name with the arguments as its JSON body, and answers with its result. Before response streaming starts, a refused call answers 409 with its `error`. A malformed call, with arguments outside the tool's schema or a filter value that names no block, floor, ore, item, liquid, unit type or team, answers 400, applies nothing, and its `error` names the field that is wrong. A long-running call may start a keepalive stream with HTTP 200 before its result is known; a later failure is reported in the JSON body's `error`. Always check that field as well as the HTTP status.
   35| 
   36| - `query {of, filter?, area?, fields?, limit?, after?}` reads current records. `{of:"collections"}` lists the collections and their fields; `{of:"ops"}` lists every input `act` accepts. A filter is `[[path, op, value], ...]`, all of which must hold, with op `eq`, `ne`, `in`, `not_in`, `lt`, `le`, `gt`, `ge` or `contains`. `area` is `{x, y, w, h}` in tiles. A reply holds up to 128 rows unless you set a larger `limit`; pass `next` as `after` to continue.
   37| - `events` reads your log of this game: native game events, the messages you can see, and your team's accepted inputs.    38| - `act {ops:[...]}` applies inputs in order and answers with one result per input; a refused input does not refuse the call. Each result has `ok` and an `effect` or an `error` (`code`, `message`, `facts`). An accepted input has started, not finished.
