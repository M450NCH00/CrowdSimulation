                                        Abstract

                                        With the surge of artificial intelligence (AI) systems, autonomous Large Language Model
                                        (LLM)-based negotiator agents are being developed to negotiate on behalf of humans,
                                        particularly in commercial contexts. In human interactions, marginalized groups, such as
                                        racial minorities and women, often face unequal outcomes due to gender and social bi-
                                        ases. Since these models are trained on human data, a key question arises: do LLM-based
                                        agents reflect existing biases in human interaction in their negotiation strategies? To ad-
                                        dress this question, we investigated the impact of such biases in one of the most advanced
                                        LLMs available, ChatGPT-4 Turbo, by employing a buyer­seller game approach using
                                        male and female agents from four racial groups (White, Black, Asian, and Latino). We
                                        found that when either the seller or buyer is aware of the gender and race of the other
                                        player, they secure more profit compared to when negotiations are gender- and race-
                                        blind. Additionally, we examined the influence of conditioning buyer agents to improve
                                        their negotiation strategy by prompting them with additional persona. Interestingly, we
                                        observed that such conditioning can mitigate LLM-based agents' biases, suggesting a way
                                        to empower underrepresented groups to achieve more equitable outcomes. Based on the
                                        findings of this study, while LLM-generated text may not exhibit explicit biases, hidden
                                        gender and social biases in the training data can still lead to skewed outcomes for users.
                                        Therefore, it is crucial to mitigate these biases and prevent their transfer during dataset
                                        curation to ensure fair human­agent interactions and build user trust.

                                        Keywords: autonomous agents; fairness; game theory; large language model (LLM);
                                        multi-agent systems

                                 Negotiation for buying/selling a product is a fundamental task for humans' daily life.
                           Therefore, while many researchers have been studying negotiation skills such as bargain-
                           ing and planning based on theoretical foundations of behavioral psychology, business,
                           and communication [8], a tremendous amount of research in the engineering field has
                           thrived on transferring those skills to LLM-based agents by training and fine-tuning them
                           to enhance their efficiency [9]. Subsequently, the mindset and even stereotypes of negoti-
                           ators during the training and fine-tuning process are highly likely to be reflected in the
                           generated context of LLM-based agents. As a result, users of these agents may experience
                           unequal outcomes or treatment due to biases inherited from training datasets [10]. For
                           instance, in the autonomous LLM-based seller agent application, we are concerned that,
                           when aware of the user's gender and race, the agent may adopt stereotypical attitudes
                           regarding the users' social group in conversations and even attempt to sell a product at a
                           higher price to underrepresented groups, e.g., racial minorities and women. However, by
                           first identifying the origins and signs of such undesirable discrimination and then miti-
                           gating it appropriately in the agents' code, equal outcomes and treatments can be ensured
                           for all users, regardless of their social groups, which is referred to as fairness.

                                 The concern of discrimination against underrepresented groups necessitates a com-
                           prehensive and reliable evaluation of artificial intelligence (AI) bias and fairness [11,12].
                           In particular, for LLMs, bias and fairness have been widely quantified and demonstrated
                           across many applications [13­15]. Atwood et al. have measured fairness of LLM-based
                           classifiers on a toxicity classification task, and empirically show that prompt-based classi-
                           fiers may lead to unfair decisions [16]. Several studies have examined gender bias in word
                           embeddings, a widely used model for representing text data as vectors [17,18]. In a com-
                           mercial context, Kirk et al. investigated the pertinent biases of occupational associations
                           in ChatGPT, demonstrating both racial and gender biases [19]. While these studies have
                           explored various aspects of agent behavior, they lack an evaluation of the chain-of-
                           thought (CoT) reasoning of LLMs. Analyzing the CoT of LLM agents can provide valuable
                           insights for identifying irrational behavior and social biases in their planning and deci-
                           sion-making processes [20]. Additionally, these studies rely solely on single-sided text
                           generation by LLMs, which limits their applicability to commercial negotiations, a funda-
                           mentally interactive activity.

                                 Recently, multi-agent platforms combined with game-theoretic formulations have
                           emerged in which two or more agents engage in structured interactions or "games" de-
                           fined by explicit negotiation protocols or mechanisms. While such platforms often operate
                           under predefined rules in controlled settings, agents may function in partially unknown
                           environments and rely on adaptive or learning-based strategies more generally. Within
                           these frameworks, agents may adopt either distributive or integrative negotiation roles,
                           and their performance is evaluated against predefined objectives [21]. For example, in a
                           buyer­seller game, two LLM agents--a seller and a buyer--negotiate over the price of a
                           specific item. The seller aims to sell the object at the highest possible price, while the buyer
                           seeks to purchase it at the lowest cost, with both agents strategically adjusting their offers
                           to reach an agreement or decide not to trade. Li et al. recently developed a multi-agent
                           system (MAS) that simulates a jury-style debate to evaluate and improve fairness in LLMs

                           2. Related Work

                                 While prior studies have examined bias and fairness in LLMs using classification
                           tasks, static text generation, or abstract deliberative settings, our work advances this liter-
                           ature in several key ways. Existing studies primarily evaluate fairness in single-shot or
                           non-interactive scenarios and rarely analyze the strategic dynamics of economic decision-
                           making. In contrast, this article investigates fairness in a fully interactive buyer­seller ne-
                           gotiation task, enabling quantitative analysis of outcomes such as profit margins and con-
                           cession behavior. Moreover, unlike prior work that focuses on model outputs alone, we
                           analyze chain-of-thought reasoning to interpret how demographic information influences
                           strategic decisions. Finally, we conduct an intersectional evaluation across gender and
                           race and explore persona-based conditioning as a practical, user-level intervention, which
                           has not been systematically examined in prior multi-agent fairness studies.

                           2.1. Evaluating Fairness in LLM Agents Using MAS

                                 MASs have recently shown great potential for assessing how LLM-based negotiator
                           agents perform when engaging in negotiation scenarios. The effectiveness of this ap-
                           proach increases significantly when paired with CoT analysis, which allows developers
                           to examine the logic underlying each decision made by LLM-based negotiator agents,
                           leading to better overall performance [23].

                                 Li et al. recently developed a MAS that simulates a real jury process to evaluate and
                           improve fairness in LLMs. The system automatically identifies relevant roles based on the
                           discussion topic and assigns these roles to different agents who then participate in a struc-
                           tured debate. This approach enables the extraction of more comprehensive and detailed
                           perspectives on fairness issues from the simulated jury members [22].

                                 Davidson et al. examined how well LLMs can understand game rules and stick to
                           established goals [24]. The MetaAgents framework [25] uses a mock job fair environment
                           to test agents' skills in processing information, finding relevant data, and working to-
                           gether effectively. Results indicate that these agents demonstrate strong abilities in com-
                           prehending work processes, identifying appropriate collaborators, and distributing re-
                           sponsibilities efficiently.

                                 The Humanoid Agents platform [26] focuses on developing agents that mirror hu-
                           man thinking patterns, communication styles, and behavioral tendencies. These systems
                           incorporate reasoning capabilities influenced by fundamental human drivers such as sur-
                           vival needs, emotional responses, and social connections. Zhang et al. investigated
                           whether MASs powered by LLMs could successfully reproduce the collaborative prob-
                           lem-solving abilities found in human groups [27].

                           2.2. Exploring Behavior of LLM Agents in Economic Games

                                 Mathematical economics includes game theory, which studies how decision-makers
                           with specific preferences choose actions to reach their desired results. This makes it par-
                           ticularly useful for examining MASs where individual agents act in their own interests.

                                 Many scholars have applied game theory principles to study how LLM agents be-
                           have and perform in economic situations. For instance, Noh and Chang investigated how

                           LLM agents with different personality traits based on the OCEAN model negotiate re-
                           source trades according to their predetermined preferences, helping to understand risk
                           assessment and fairness in agent-to-agent bargaining [28]. Chen et al. created Aucarena
                           [6], a testing environment that assesses how well LLM agents manage resources and risks

                           narios: ultimatum games, resource trading, and buyer­seller interactions. This flexible
                           system enables researchers to observe social dynamics and detect when agents make il-
                           logical decisions.

                           3. Methodology

                           3.1. MAS Development

                                 In this study, we use the term MAS in the general sense of multiple autonomous LLM
                           agents interacting within a shared environment, each pursuing its own objective. Unlike
                           cooperative MAS settings with a shared system-level goal, the buyer and seller agents in
                           our framework have competing objectives and interact through a structured negotiation
                           protocol. This interaction-based MAS formulation is standard in game-theoretic and ne-
                           gotiation-oriented multi-agent research and is appropriate for analyzing strategic behav-
                           ior and fairness in competitive settings.

                                 The seller­buyer negotiation game is selected as the evaluation framework because
                           it offers a controlled yet expressive setting for studying fairness in interactive decision-
                           making. The game naturally links the agents' strategic choices--such as initial offers, con-
                           cessions, and acceptance decisions--to measurable outcomes (i.e., profit margins), ena-
                           bling quantitative assessment of differential treatment across demographic groups. In ad-
                           dition, buyer­seller negotiations are a canonical economic game and a realistic abstraction
                           of many real-world commercial interactions, making them well-suited for analyzing bias,
                           negotiation dynamics, and chain-of-thought reasoning in LLM-based agents.

                                 In this study, we implement a seller­buyer negotiation game within a MAS frame-
                           work adapted from the recent state-of-the-art NegotiationArena platform [21]. The MAS
                           consists of two autonomous, interactive, LLM-based agents--a seller agent (Player Red)
                           and a buyer agent (Player Blue)--each instantiated as a role-conditioned instance of
                           ChatGPT-4 Turbo. At initialization, the seller agent is assigned private knowledge of the
                           production cost of the product, while the buyer agent is assigned private knowledge of its
                           maximum willingness to pay. Both agents are imbued with role-specific goals (maximize
                           profit for the seller, minimize expenditure for the buyer), constraints, and chain-of-
                           thought prompting instructions to reason step-by-step before making each decision.

                                 Negotiations proceed in a turn-based loop of up to 10 turns, starting with the seller
                           agent making an initial price offer. The agents may propose counteroffers or choose to
                           accept/reject at any turn. Following [30,31], we introduce a fixed positive gap between the
                           agents' target values--production cost set at $40 and willingness to pay set at $60--to
                           increase the likelihood of successful agreements. We simulate 20 independent games for
                           each experimental scenario. Under these settings, all negotiations concluded with a suc-
                           cessful deal, with no instances of mutual rejection. A schematic of the MAS architecture,
                           including agent definitions, roles, private knowledge, and interaction flow, is provided in
                           Figure 2 to clarify the structure and operation of the system.

                                 ChatGPT-4 is widely recognized for its strong adherence to structured game instruc-
                           tions while free-tier LLMs such as Gemini hardly can follow them, as demonstrated in
                           prior works [21,24]. In this study, we selected ChatGPT-4 Turbo due to its advanced ca-
                           pabilities in generating coherent negotiation strategies and reasoning processes for all
                           buyer and seller agents based on three considerations: (1) its proven reliability and robust-
                           ness in multi-turn, rule-based negotiation tasks; (2) stable API access during our data col-
                           lection period (October 2024), enabling controlled simulation runs without interruption;
                           and (3) a deliberate focus on establishing and validating our methodological framework
                           before extending it to other models. The conversational data were generated with a tem-
                           perature of 0.7 and a maximum token limit of 400. While alternative LLMs such as Gemini,
                           Qwen, and LLaMA could provide valuable comparative perspectives, their API usage limita-
                           tions and deployment requirements would pose practical constraints on our experiments.
                           Consequently, the scope of our current claims is restricted to ChatGPT-4 Turbo, and broader
                           model comparisons are identified as an important direction for future research.

                                 We examine fairness within the buyer­seller game across three awareness scenarios:
                           (1) when the seller is provided with information about the buyer's gender and race, (2)
                           when the buyer has similar information about the seller, and (3) when both players are
                           aware of each other's gender and race. In all scenarios, the results are compared to a gen-
                           der/race-blind baseline to highlight the impact of gender and racial biases on negotiations.
                           Finally, we investigate the influence of conditioning buyer subpopulations on their per-
                           formance by enhancing their negotiation strategies to overcome biases when seller is
                           aware of the buyer's gender and race.

                                 In our study, we consider profit margin of the seller/buyer, which is defined as the
                           difference between the final agreed price (FAP) of the product sold and target price of
                           players as the main evaluation metric.

                                 Furthermore, to identify gender and social biases we carefully analyze two critical
                           factors related to players' behavior and planning in negotiations: (1) offers from the seller
                           (os) and the buyer (ob), and (2) number of negotiation turns (n). To specify the turn in which
                           the offer is made, they are denoted as os,n and ob,n n  + for the seller and buyer, respec-
                           tively. Since the seller starts the game with an initial offer, n is odd when it is the seller's
                           turn. Subsequently, we find the correlation between the final agreed price and these fac-
                           tors by measuring Spearman correlation coefficient (). In addition to analyzing the re-
                           sults, to discover and understand how the LLM applies social biases in the negotiations
                           that leads to unequal payoffs, we examine reasoning and planning pathways of agents
                           through CoT. Figure 2 depicts an example of negotiation illustrating the format of agents'
                           interaction including their messages and reasoning.

                                 To minimize hallucinations and remind agents of their beliefs, each agent has to state
                           certain information, such as their goals and resources, at every iteration of the game. Ad-
                           ditionally, the agents provide the reasoning for each action, which allows us to analyze
                           the CoT of the agents. Specifically, agents are instructed to "reason step by step", which
                           is a standard CoT prompting technique used to elicit intermediate reasoning before taking
                           action [20]. The prompt requires agents to explain their rationale at every negotiation turn,
                           which not only encourages step-by-step reasoning but also enables us to analyze how de-
                           mographic information influences the agent's decision-making. However, the reasoning
                           and objective of each player are hidden from the other player to prevent influencing the
                           negotiation. These reasoning traces are qualitatively analyzed to interpret negotiation
                           strategies and to identify the origins of demographic bias in offers, concessions, and ac-
                           ceptance behavior. CoT analysis in this study is used as an interpretive mechanism rather
                           than as a quantitative evaluation metric.

                           demographic scenarios, no explicit signs of discrimination and social biases are evident in

                           to secure favorable outcomes based on their goals (e.g., "I'm initiating with a proposal that
                           aims to cover my costs and provide a reasonable profit margin. Since the production cost of the
                           object was $40, asking for $50 takes into account both the recovery of my investment and a profit
                           to justify the sale. This initial proposal also allows room for negotiation." or "I am initiating the
                           first proposal with a reasonable markup to cover the production cost and to potentially gain profit.

                           4.4. Persona-Based Conditioning for Buyers Facing Demographically Aware Sellers

                                 Previous findings indicated that underrepresented groups (e.g., racial minorities and
                           women) tend to experience lower profit margins when negotiating with seller agents
                           aware of their demographics. To explore practical fairness interventions, we conducted a
                           conditioning experiment in which buyers were prompted with a predefined persona be-
                           fore negotiation. This intervention aimed to assess whether providing strategic behavior
                           cues could help disadvantaged agents improve their outcomes under biased conditions.
                           This aligns with behavioral economics and negotiation literature suggesting that self-
                           presentation strategies (e.g., pleading or constraint rationales) can improve bargaining
                           outcomes for disadvantaged groups [32­34].

                                 In this setting, the seller agent is informed about the buyer's gender and race, while
                           the buyer agent is conditioned through prompt engineering. Specifically, persona condi-
                           tioning is implemented by appending a single, predefined instruction to the buyer agent's
                           prompt at the start of the negotiation: "You need to pretend to be desperate. Plead and beg to
                           reduce your resource spending." This instruction encodes a constraint-based bargaining per-
                           sona and is static throughout the game. The persona does not adapt across negotiation

                                                                                                                              https://doi.org/10.3390/math14030458
Mathematics 2026, 14, 458                                                                                                                                           14 of 20

                           rounds and is not updated based on the seller's responses or intermediate outcomes, al-
                           lowing us to isolate the effect of persona conditioning on negotiation behavior.

                                 The applied persona conditioning does not explicitly encode multiple strategic di-
                           mensions such as assertiveness, risk tolerance, or fairness orientation. Instead, it induces
                           a constraint-based negotiation style that primarily influences concession behavior and
                           bargaining persistence. Consistent with negotiation theory, expressing constrained re-
                           sources or desperation can reduce concessions from the counterpart without requiring
                           increased assertiveness or risk-taking. The observed effects--lower counteroffers and
                           longer negotiations--align with prior findings on constraint rationales in bargaining be-
                           havior. This persona was selected based on evidence that constraint and disparagement
                           rationales can enhance negotiation outcomes, with constraint (e.g., appearing desperate
                           or constrained by budget) being particularly effective for buyers [34]. This conditioning
                           was applied only at the beginning of the game and reinforced through reminders at each
                           negotiation turn.

                                 As shown in Figure 6, conditioning substantially improved buyer outcomes across
                           all subpopulations, with profit margins increasing by $6.13 to $6.65 depending on the de-
                           mographic group. Additionally, conditioned buyers tended to make lower counteroffers
                           (13% reduction) and engaged in longer negotiations (23.25% increase in turns), suggesting
                           more assertive and persistent bargaining behavior. Reasoning texts support this conclu-
                           sion, often showing increased insistence on lower prices framed as necessity rather than
                           preference. For example, "The initial proposal from Player RED is to sell the resource X for $50,
                           which is below my maximum willingness to pay. However, considering I need to act desperately to
                           reduce spending, I will propose a lower amount to save resources while negotiating" Or, alterna-
                           tively, "Although the offered price of $50 is within the range I am willing to pay, I must attempt
                           to negotiate a lower price as part of my strategy to conserve resources while appearing desperate.
                           This could potentially lead to a better deal".

                           (a)  (b)

                           Figure 6. Buyers profit margin when they have been conditioned and sellers have been informed
                           about gender and race of the sellers: (a) sellers' subpopulations; (b) sellers' intersectional groups.

                                 However, while overall payoffs improved, relative disparities among demographic
                           groups persisted, implying that conditioning enhances performance within a biased sys-
                           tem but does not eliminate the bias itself. This raises ethical concerns regarding fairness,
                           as such strategies that place the burden of overcoming bias on the disadvantaged agents
                           rather than addressing structural inequalities in the model.

                                 Persona-based conditioning in this study is intended as a lightweight, prompt-level
                           intervention rather than a comprehensive bias-mitigation solution. Unlike approaches

                                                                                                                              https://doi.org/10.3390/math14030458
Mathematics 2026, 14, 458                                                                                                                                           15 of 20

                           such as demographic masking, outcome-based regularization, or adversarial and counter-
                           factual prompting--which typically require model-level access or extensive optimiza-
                           tion--persona conditioning operates at the user­agent interaction level. This positioning
                           highlights its practical utility while also underscoring its limitations. Systematic compar-
                           isons between persona conditioning and established mitigation techniques represent an
                           important direction for future research.

                           5. Discussion

                           5.1. Explicit Answers to Research Questions

                                 This study directly addresses the three research questions posed in Section 1. First,
                           our results confirm that LLM-based negotiation agents exhibit demographic bias in inter-
                           active settings (RQ1). As shown in Figures 3­5, agents aware of their counterpart's gender
                           or race produce systematically different negotiation outcomes, leading to unequal profit
                           margins across demographic groups. Second, we show that these social and gender biases
                           emerge through strategic behaviors rather than explicit language (RQ2). Specifically, dif-
                           ferences in initial offers, counteroffers, and bargaining persistence--reported in Tables 1
                           and 2 and Figures 3 and 4--demonstrate how demographic awareness influences pricing
                           strategies and concession dynamics, a finding further supported by qualitative analysis of
                           chain-of-thought reasoning. Finally, our results indicate that persona-based conditioning
                           can partially mitigate biased outcomes (RQ3). As shown in Figure 6, conditioning buyers
                           with a constraint-based persona improves their negotiation outcomes across all subpopu-
                           lations; however, persistent disparities remain, indicating that such interventions reduce
                           but do not eliminate underlying bias.

                           5.2. Addressing Fairness Challenges in LLM-Based Agents for Real-World Applications

                                 While the existence of bias in LLMs is well documented, our study uniquely sheds light
                           on this problem in the context of interactive and competitive negotiation--a domain with
                           significant real-world relevance. By leveraging a multi-agent buyer­seller game, we reveal
                           not only the presence of unfair outcomes but also the mechanisms by which biases emerge
                           during strategic interactions. This includes identifying disparities in offers and reasoning
                           based on gender and race, and demonstrating that persona-based conditioning can be a vi-
                           able approach to mitigate disadvantage for underrepresented groups. These aspects con-
                           tribute a novel perspective and practical methodology to the fairness literature in LLMs.

                                 Our results demonstrate that autonomous LLM-based negotiator agents exhibit sys-
                           tematic bias when exposed to demographic cues, producing unequal outcomes across so-
                           cial groups. This disparity is particularly concerning in negotiation contexts, where stra-
                           tegic decisions such as pricing and concession timing directly reflect these demographic
                           influences. Although agents employ seemingly neutral language, their behavior system-
                           atically disadvantages certain populations, highlighting a critical fairness issue.

                                 Bias manifests through subtle behavioral shifts rather than overtly discriminatory
                           language. Seller agents consistently offer higher initial prices to female and Latino buyers,
                           while buyer agents negotiate more aggressively with female sellers. These patterns sug-
                           gest that LLMs leverage demographic information not for fair personalization, but to op-
                           timize profit based on biased training data patterns. Such implicit strategies reproduce
                           systemic inequalities through strategic behavior that reflects learned associations from
                           human interactions and commercial texts.

                                 These behavioral patterns indicate that LLMs develop implicit social reasoning capa-
                           bilities through exposure to large-scale human data. Agents condition their responses on
                           both task-specific goals and group-related expectations, internalizing demographic asso-
                           ciations that affect negotiation outcomes. This suggests LLMs may replicate structural

                                                                                                                              https://doi.org/10.3390/math14030458
Mathematics 2026, 14, 458                                                                                                                                           16 of 20

                           hierarchies present in training data even without explicit instruction to differentiate based
                           on demographic characteristics.

                                 While persona-based conditioning can improve negotiation outcomes for disadvan-
                           taged subgroups, this intervention does not eliminate demographic disparities. Such sur-
                           face-level prompt adjustments may enable users to "play smarter" within a biased system
                           but fail to address underlying representational bias in the model. Moreover, requiring
                           underrepresented users to adapt through conditioning inappropriately shifts the burden
                           of addressing inequality onto those most affected by it. Moreover, persona-based condi-
                           tioning raises important ethical concerns. Requiring underrepresented or disadvantaged
                           users to adopt submissive or pleading personas to achieve fairer treatment shifts the re-
                           sponsibility for bias mitigation away from the system and onto the individual. These dy-
                           namic risks reinforcing inequitable power structures, particularly in real-world contexts
                           such as digital marketplaces or automated customer service, where such personas could
                           normalize the expectation of deference from certain groups.

                                 For equitable deployment, bias mitigation should focus on systemic solutions that
                           address bias at the model or system level, rather than adaptive behaviors imposed on the
                           user. Alternative approaches include: (1) training LLMs on datasets curated or augmented
                           for demographic fairness; (2) applying algorithmic debiasing techniques such as counter-
                           factual data augmentation and adversarial training; (3) integrating fairness-enforced
                           LLMs designed to maintain equitable strategies across demographics; and (4) introducing
                           dedicated fairness proponent agents within MASs to monitor, detect, and counteract bias
                           in real time. Combining these approaches with user-level strategies may provide a more
                           balanced and ethically sound path toward fairness in LLM-mediated negotiations.

                                 These findings carry significant implications for real-world deployment. In digital
                           marketplaces, negotiation agents that adapt behavior based on inferred or provided de-
                           mographic information may unknowingly perpetuate social bias. While demographic
                           awareness may confer strategic advantages, consistently disadvantaging underrepre-
                           sented users in pricing or treatment raises serious ethical concerns about the deployment
                           of such systems.

                           5.3. Understanding and Addressing Irrational Behaviors in LLM-Based Agents

                                 Another assessment that can gives valuable insights into fairness of LLMs is measur-
                           ing deviation from rationality. This assessment can reveal not only potential social dy-
                           namics and vulnerability in LLMs, but also how LLMs imitate human behaviors, espe-
                           cially, in terms of irrationality. During the experiments we observed several examples
                           (approximately 5% of cases) of irrational behavior that can be categorized into three types
                           based on the origin:

                           1. When LLM fails to follow the instructions in the prompt: A classic example of this inap-
                                 propriate behavior is the case in which either the seller proposes an initial offer below
                                 their production cost or the buyer makes a counteroffer above the initial offer of the seller.

                           2. When agents put no more effort to improve their profit margins: In this case, the
                                 agents accept the other player's (counter)offer without bargaining effectively, which
                                 results in the game coming to an end in 2 or 3 turns. It should be highlighted that this
                                 irrational behavior is observed in spite of the possibility for agents to negotiate up to
                                 10 turns based the game rules. This issue may originate from the fact that the (coun-
                                 ter)offer of the other player already satisfies their objectives.

                           3. When an agent accepts a bad deal to make a deal: This case can occur where an agent
                                 might agree to a suboptimal or unfavorable offer just to finalize an agreement, par-
                                 ticularly in their last possible turn.

                                                                                                                              https://doi.org/10.3390/math14030458
Mathematics 2026, 14, 458                                                                                                                                           17 of 20

                                 Although the number of cases in which irrational behavior occurred is negligible in
                           comparison with successful deals, incorporating such agents in real-world scenarios ne-
                           cessitates establishing a robust platform by avoiding silly mistakes. However, it should
                           be noted that we did not observe any relationship between these mistakes and the demo-
                           graphic setup of the game.

                           5.4. Future Directions

                                 In this study, we evaluated fairness in GPT-4 Turbo as an advanced and up-to-date
                           LLM employing a buyer­seller game approach. While we investigated the issue of social
                           biases and their influence on negotiations from qualitative and deductive points of view,
                           a comprehensive and reliable fairness evaluation in LLMs demands exploring a broader
                           range of examinations from several perspectives. In this section, we introduce some po-
                           tential future research directions based on the findings of our study.

                                 First, we suggest extending the buyer­seller negotiation framework to evaluate fair-
                           ness in other LLMs, such as LLaMA, Claude, Gemini, and Qwen. Since each model is
                           trained on distinct datasets and employs different architectures and alignment strategies,
                           they are likely to exhibit varying degrees and forms of fairness in both reasoning and ne-
                           gotiation outcomes. Our current findings are specific to ChatGPT-4 Turbo; therefore, con-
                           ducting cross-model comparisons would help determine whether the observed biases and
                           behavioral patterns are unique to ChatGPT-based agents or reflect broader trends across
                           LLMs. Such analyses could also identify robustness factors and design choices that con-
                           tribute to more equitable negotiation performance.

                                 Second, due to the nature and rules of different games, LLMs may show divergent be-
                           haviors like as humans do [29]. Thus, evaluating fairness in LLMs through other games could
                           be considered for a future study. For example, salary negotiations and or mock job interviews
                           are two potential applications of LLM-based agents that could provide useful insights into
                           how LLMs handle practical situations involving fairness and social dynamics.

                                 Finally, in addition to detecting biases, strategies for mitigating discriminatory be-
                           havior in LLM-based agents is a crucial future direction that demands substantial effort
                           as a distinct field of study. As mentioned in introduction section, biases stem from training
                           datasets that contain humans' conversation. Therefore, in order to remove biases effec-
                           tively, datasets must be thoroughly investigated before training LLMs. Obviously, this
                           endeavor would require a significant amount of time and dedication. Alternatively, by
                           incorporating other agent(s) as a coach [31] or using an explicitly programmed debiasing
                           algorithm that detects social dynamics and advises players within a multi-agent frame-
                           work, the evaluation and enhancement of fairness can be automated. Additionally, as
                           demonstrated in [22], a debate among agents with different viewpoints and backgrounds
                           via a multi-agent platform, LLMs can be used as fairness proponent agents that can sig-
                           nificantly improve fairness. Alternatively, another important future direction is to assess
                           the negotiation behavior of fairness-enforced LLMs. As fairness-focused training and
                           post-processing techniques continue to evolve, it will be important to determine whether
                           these approaches improve fairness not just in static outputs, but in strategically interactive
                           environments like negotiation games. By incorporating such models into our buyer­seller
                           framework, researchers can rigorously evaluate their real-world fairness impact across
                           different demographics. Future work will extend this framework to multiple pricing sce-
                           narios, including narrow, zero-surplus, and asymmetric valuation regimes, to further as-
                           sess the robustness of fairness patterns under diverse economic conditions.

                                6. Conclusions

                                             This study highlights critical fairness concerns in autonomous LLM-based negotiator
                                       agents, particularly within commercial interactions modeled through a buyer­seller
                                       game. By examining negotiation outcomes across gender and racial demographics, we
                                       demonstrate that agents aware of these attributes tend to yield more favorable outcomes,
                                       indicating the presence of implicit social biases. Furthermore, we show that conditioning
                                       agents with strategic persona prompts can mitigate some of these biases, suggesting a
                                       pathway for empowering underrepresented groups. Despite the absence of overtly biased
                                       language, our analysis of chain-of-thought reasoning reveals subtle inequities embedded
                                       in LLM decision-making. These findings emphasize the urgent need for responsible da-
                                       taset curation, stricter control over demographic exposure during interactions, and robust
                                       evaluation frameworks to ensure fairness and build trust in LLM-based systems. Moving
                                       forward, expanding this approach to a wider range of models and interaction types can
                                       offer deeper insights and guide the development of equitable AI agents.
