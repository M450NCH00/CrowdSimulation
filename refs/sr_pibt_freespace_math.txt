                                       Abstract

                                       Free-space multi-agent path planning remains challenging at large scales. Most existing
                                       methods either offer optimality guarantees but do not scale beyond a few dozen agents
                                       or rely on grid-world assumptions that do not generalize well to continuous space. In
                                       this paper, we propose a hybrid, rule-based planning framework that combines Priority
                                       Inheritance with Backtracking (PIBT) with a novel safety-aware path smoothing method.
                                       Our approach extends PiBT to eight-connected grids and selectively applies string-pulling-
                                       based smoothing while preserving collision safety through local interaction awareness and
                                       a fallback collision resolution step based on Safe Interval Path Planning (SIPP). This design
                                       allows us to reduce overall path lengths while maintaining real-time performance. We
                                       demonstrate that our method can scale to over 500 agents in large free-space environments,
                                       outperforming existing any-angle and optimal methods in terms of runtime, while produc-
                                       ing near-optimal trajectories in sparse domains. Our results suggest this framework is a
                                       promising building block for scalable, real-time multi-agent navigation in robotics systems
                                       operating beyond grid constraints.

                                       Keywords: multi-agent systems; path planning; path smoothing; multi-agent pathfinding

                            2. Related Work

                            2.1. Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF)

                                  Multi-agent path finding (MAPF) is a vibrant domain. A lot of work has focused on
                            solving MAPF optimally. Specifically, the community has targeted grid-like settings as
                            these are good enough approximations of the real world warehouse settings and a good
                            starting point for research. Currently there are several families of MAPF algorithms that
                            can be broadly broken down into the following:

                            · Conflict-Based Search (CBS) [4]: These algorithms rely on a two-level search. The
                                  low-level search runs traditional path-finding algorithms. When a conflict arises,
                                  the high level search creates child nodes in a tree that split on the specific conflict.
                                  This approach has been shown to be very effective in the 10­100-robot setting [5].
                                  Additionally, its simplicity has lent itself to many extensions [5,6]. However, it often
                                  suffers from exploring too many conflicts and may have unpredictable runtimes [7,8].

                            · Compilation-Based: Compilation-based methods decompose the MAPF problem to
                                  equivalent NP-hard problems such as MILP [9] or SAT [10]. This approach has limited
                                  scalability and is hard to extend [9].

                            · Rules-Based: This category of solvers relies on bespoke hand-engineered heuristics.
                                  A recent breakthrough by Keisuke et al. [1] introduced PIBT as a viable rule-based
                                  approach to multi-agent solving with practical scalability.

                            · Priority-Based: These are the simplest solvers and scale well; however, they often run
                                  into deadlocks [1].

                            · Learning-Based: Learning-based approaches such as PRIMAL [11], SCRIMP [12] and
                                  MAPF-GPT [13] are growing in popularity in part due to the recent advances in Deep
                                  Reinforcement Learning (RL) and improved GPU performance. Most learning-based
                                  planners need to be bootstrapped via imitation learning from other types of planners,
                                  and then they refine their strategies via RL. They are useful for decentralized, partially
                                  observable local behaviors [11­13].

                                  Aside from these broad categories, there are many varieties of planners that explore
                            hybrid strategies. We chose to work with PIBT as it can be used as a component by other
                            planners and provides unprecedented scalability. Grid-world representations have been
                            a central focus of MAPF research. Several standard benchmarks exist for these [14,15].
                            These provide an excellent but simple base for researching new search schemes. However,
                            many real-world robotics scenarios would benefit from not being constrained to the re-
                            strictive four-connected grid approach. Adreychuk et al. [2] proposed AA-CCBS, which

                                                                                                                            https://doi.org/10.3390/math14071195
Mathematics 2026, 14, 1195                                                                                                                                              3 of 14

                            builds on CCBS and is combined with SIPP to achieve optimal any-angle planning. A
                            similar approach was attempted by Open-RMF's MAPF library as well [16]. While these
                            provide optimality guarantees, many applications prefer a solution to be available (even if
                            suboptimal) than having to wait for a solver to arrive at the optimal solution.

                                  PIBT was proposed by Keisuke et al. as a "Configuration Generator" for MAPF solvers.
                            It provides a certain simple set of rules that gives feasible configurations quickly. PIBT
                            combines the notion of priority-based planning together with deadlock resolution methods.
                            Specifically, PIBT uses priority inheritance to prevent priority inversion and backtracking
                            to resolve deadlocks. It has been proven to be complete for the multi-agent pickup and
                            delivery (MAPD) problem on bi-connected graphs [1]. Its speed makes it practical for many
                            modern MAPF planners to use as a low-level planner [8,17].

                            2.2. Path Smoothing and Single-Agent Navigation
                                  When dealing with free-space navigation, theta* is commonly used for any-angle

                            path finding [18]. However, theta* can be expensive as it needs to perform line-of-sight
                            checks [19]. Several approaches to solve this have been proposed, including lazy-theta* [19].
                            For our purpose, it was more important to be able to scale to more robots than to pro-
                            vide perfect optimality. This is important for large-scale applications as optimal MAPF
                            is NP-hard. Suboptimal MAPF is believed to be solvable in poly-time. In fact, PIBT
                            has O(|A|((G) + logA)) time complexity [1] per times tep. In conventional navigation,
                            like Nav2, it is a common practice to use path planners that do not guarantee any-angle
                            optimality and then smooth the paths [3] post hoc. Some common path smoothers in-
                            clude Savitzky­Golay, string pulling [20] and constraint path smoothing [3]. However,
                            while these methods are effective for single-agent navigation where obstacles are largely
                            static, they are often "safety-blind" in multi-agent contexts. The challenge with post hoc
                            smoothing in MAPF is that it frequently introduces unintended side effects: a "straight-line"
                            optimization that is safe in isolation may result in spatiotemporal collisions when other
                            agents simultaneously adjust their own trajectories [21]. Traditional solutions to this prob-
                            lem involve either global re-optimization, which is computationally expensive and poorly
                            suited for hundreds of agents, or relying on local reactive avoidance (like ORCA [22]),
                            which can lead to oscillations or deadlocks in dense areas. We propose a procedure that
                            significantly reduces the number of intersections by proactively identifying and preserving
                            critical interaction segments, thus bridging the gap between efficient post hoc smoothing
                            and multi-agent safety.

                            2.3. Eight-Connected Grid--A Good Enough Approximation
                                  There are many ways to represent a free space. In the domain of single agent planning,

                            it is common to use a grid. When planning for one agent, visibility graphs or theta* can
                            accelerate optimal path finding. However, the improvement that theta* has over classic
                            eight-connected grids is roughly 8% [23].

                            3. Proposed Algorithm

                                  We propose a path smoother that guarantees collision-free paths while reusing the
                            results of eight-connected PIBT.

                            3.1. Eight-Connected PIBT
                                  There are two possible ways to extend PIBT to the 8-connected case: The first is to

                            simply extend the "get neighbors" function and update the collision check to take into
                            account the diagonal cases. This is shown in Figure 1. The second more complicated way
                            would be to extend priority inheritance over multiple cells. For this work we only consider
                            the first approach.

                                  Despite a speed penalty for the higher branching factor, we find 8-connected PIBT
                            is able to scale to 100 s of robots. Additionally, we find paths proposed by 8-connected
                            PIBT to be shorter than their 4-connected counter parts. One of the pitfalls of 8-connected
                            PIBT however is that the travel time in the diagonal is not the same as the travel time in
                            the cardinal directions. For the purpose of this paper, we assume the robots can operate
                            at a range of speeds, with the maximum speed being more than the time it takes to cover
                            a diagonal of a single cell. It can be shown that any line that is contained in a square has
                            to be shorter than or equal to the length of its diagonal; thus, we can use this as the max
                            robot speed. Using 8-connected PIBT is important as, when we perform string pulling, the
                            newly formed line that shortens the path can deviate significantly from the original path.
                            With the 4-connected variant, this can be problematic as we may aggressively pull strings,
                            leading to more collisions (see Section 4).

                            3.2. Safety-Aware String Pulling for Path Smoothing

                                  The key insight our algorithm uses is that PIBT negotiations are local; hence, we hand
                            control to PIBT when agents are directly interacting and hand control to our smoother when
                            agents are not directly interacting, as illustrated in Figure 2.

                                  String pulling is a classic technique for shortening paths produced by A* [20]. However,
                            when dealing with free-space planning and the output of PIBT, it is important to take into
                            account how agents interact. In this section we directly borrow terminology from the
                            original PIBT paper [1]. The interaction of agents is governed by the notion of interacting
                            agents. Interacting agents are defined by the fact that PIBT is a local-first configuration
                            generator. If two agents are located within two hops of each other, they are considered to be
                            directly interacting. This two-hop threshold is chosen because the backtracking mechanism
                            in PIBT typically resolves conflicts within a two-cell radius in an 8-connected grid; beyond
                            this range, the local configuration generation of one agent is decoupled from the other.
                            A group of interacting agents is defined transitively over chains of directly interacting
                            agents. It follows that every interacting agent must be directly interacting with some agent.
                            Therefore, a simple space­time search should reveal the neighbors of an agent. If there
                            are interactions, we do not attempt to smooth them. This alone does not guarantee the
                            safety of the newly smoothed path. We must ensure that our pulled string does not cross
                            into regions of critical interactions. We define an area of critical interaction as an area that is
                            within 1 hop of another agentm, as the agent may enter this region in the next time step
                            and was not expecting an obstacle in the original plan (see Figure 3). This one-hop "buffer"
                            is a conservative empirical choice that aligns with the maximum displacement of an agent
                            in a single time step, ensuring that the smoothed path does not intersect the reachable set
                            of any neighboring agent during the next control cycle.

                                  We refer our reader to Algorithm 1. If an agent is an interacting agent, we make sure
                            the agent reaches the critical interaction at the allotted time, and we do not perform any
                            smoothing while agents are in critical interactions (see line 31 in Algorithm 1). Our line-of-
                            sight check during string pulling takes into account regions of critical interaction by treating
                            such regions as a time-bound obstacle (line 9).

                                  Even after these restrictions, we do not have a guaranteed collision-free path. It is
                            possible that 2 newly pulled strings might intersect at the same time and place. To avoid
                            this, we rely on a complementary solver. In this case, we implement SIPP with a priority-
                            based scheme to prevent the two agents from colliding. In our benchmarks we found that
                            the number of intersections were low, and all of them were possible to resolve using SIPP.
                            However, a more complete solution would be an interesting direction for future work.

                            4. Benchmarks

                            4.1. Evaluation Criteria

                                  To rigorously evaluate the performance and scalability of our proposed framework,
                            we define the following metrics:
                            · Near-optimality: We quantify path quality by comparing the total arclength of the

                                  generated trajectories to the optimal any-angle solutions provided by AA-CCBS+SIPP,

                                                                                    https://doi.org/10.3390/math14071195
Mathematics 2026, 14, 1195                                                                                                                                              7 of 14

                                  where available. "Near-optimal" is defined as a total solution cost within a 1% margin
                                  of the optimal baseline.
                            · Makespan and temporal consistency: We monitor the makespan (the total time to
                                  reach the final goal) to ensure that the post-processing smoothing does not violate
                                  the temporal constraints established by the PIBT configuration generator. By design,
                                  our smoother preserves the original arrival times at critical interaction segments to
                                  maintain the safety guarantees of the PIBT plan.
                            · Computational efficiency: this is measured by the wall-clock time required to generate
                                  the initial PIBT plan and the subsequent smoothing and collision repair steps.

                                  Compared to AA-CCBS+SIPP, we show that our PIBT-based approach has the ability
                            to scale more effectively. As seen in Table 1, our solver can find solutions for 10 s­100 s of
                            agents in a practical timeframe, whereas AA-CCBS+SIPP can achieve free-space planning
                            for 2­15 agents. It is important to note that AA-CBS+SIPP offers optimality guarantees,
                            which we do not. However as the optimal variant of MAPF is known to be NP-hard [24],
                            in many real-world scenarios, we would prefer methods with a bounded runtime.

                                  The path-smoothing approach is particularly effective when there are large empty
                            areas with geometries that may not fit well into a grid world. This explains the extreme
                            effectiveness in the Berlin map, where path smoothing was able to reduce path length by
                            up to 27.8% of the original path length proposed by PIBT. Conversely, path smoothing has
                            almost no effect in worlds with narrow grid patterns like the warehouse world, where the
                            improvements via path smoothing are as low as 0%.

                                  The path-smoothing approach is also not appropriate for reducing the makespan as
                            the makespan of the trajectories is preserved whenever there is any critical interaction. A
                            consequence of this is shown in Table 5, where the total path length reduction decreases as
                            agent density increases. This is a mechanical consequence of our safety-aware heuristic:
                            as agents become more densely packed, larger proportions of their paths are classified
                            as critical interaction regions, reducing the "smoothable" fraction of the total trajectory.
                            To isolate the smoother's intrinsic performance from this density effect, we report the
                            "Normalized Path Length Reduction" in Table 5, which is computed only over the segments
                            that were actually subjected to smoothing. This normalized metric demonstrates that the
                            smoothing mechanism remains highly effective on available free-space segments regardless
                            of overall agent density, confirming that the declining total gain is a deliberate trade-off for
                            safety rather than a degradation of the smoother's capabilities.

                                  Perhaps the most significant drawback of our algorithm is the need to check for
                            intersections and resolve them via traditional MAPF techniques at the end of our smoothing
                            if new trajectory intersections arise. Our experiments show that such intersections do arise
                            when handling hundreds of robots; however, the number of new intersections stays well
                            below 20 in most cases. Such intersections usually arise for a limited number of robots, so
                            their resolution is relatively easy to compute, and the solutions for each intersection can be
                            computed independent from each other.

                                  Based on our findings in Table 4, we see that considering areas of critical interaction is
                            incredibly important for avoiding the need to fall back to local collision avoidance techniques.

                                  Apart from the specific cases highlighted in our discussion, we observe an average
                            path length reduction of 7.3% across all tested scenarios and had an average overhead
                            of 19% for larger instances (100 robots). The overhead was much smaller in smaller
                            instances, where it was as low as 2%.

                            6. Conclusions and Future Work

                                  We showed a method for path smoothing over PIBT. Our key insight was that we used
                            the local properties of PIBT to reduce the number of collisions introduced when smoothing
                            the path. By partitioning the path into segments where PIBT has full control due to high
                            robot density and segments that are safe to smooth, we showed that we reduced the path
                            length in maps with large amounts of free space while introducing a minimal number of
                            new trajectory intersections. This approach allowed us to approximate free space traversal
                            of robots at a much larger scale than conventional AA-CCBS+SIPP.

                                  Several factors that may be relevant to real-world applications are not modeled in our
                            method. First, we did not look at the Kino-dynamics of the robot. This can be problematic
                            if the planner introduces a particularly sharp turn or, for instance, the robot is unable to
                            come to an immediate stop. Second, our approach assumes the homogeneity of the robots.
                            In reality, not all robots will behave in the same way. Deployments may mix different types
                            of robots. This type of heterogeneity is not supported in PIBT and could be a good future
                            direction for research.

                                  We also could not reduce the makespan of a smoothed path as we relied on the conflicts
                            happening at fixed times dictated by the original plan. A future direction would be to try

                            https://doi.org/10.3390/math14071195
Mathematics 2026, 14, 1195  13 of 14

                                       to derive a semantic plan instead of a time-based plan and then apply path smoothing in a
                                       posthoc fashion. This may enable us to reduce the total makespan.

                                              Additionally, the escape hatch we used to avoid collisions at newly introduced tra-
                                       jectory intersections is less than ideal. Future work can focus on finding a way to ensure
                                       collisions do not take place. Alternatively, instead of using SIPP [25], other methods like
                                       Optimal Reciprocal Collision Avoidance (ORCA) [22] or Control Barrier Functions [26] (or,
                                       for that matter, even PIBT) can be used to resolve such collisions.

                                              Additionally, while this work focused on grid-based safety regions, exploring the
                                       robustness of the method in highly dynamic environments with moving obstacles or
                                       heterogeneous agents remains an open challenge. Future evaluations could incorporate
                                       simple dynamic constraints, such as maximum rotation angles or acceleration limits, to
                                       bring the simulated results closer to the requirements of physical robot systems.

                                              Our work also makes no claims about the optimality or completeness of our method.
                                       This is an important area for future research. Perhaps we can work such a path smoother
                                       into an optimizer like LaCAM* [8] in the future to provide optimality guarantees.
