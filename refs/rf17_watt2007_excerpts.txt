[116] On each trial the observer was shown an image from the stimulus set and was required 
[117] to indicate, by pressing a button, whether the person in the image was looking to the 
[118] left or to the right of straight ahead. The size of the cue on each trial was the angular 
[119] deviation (from straight ahead) of the eye of the person in the image. At the end of the 
[120] sequence of trials, a cumulative Gaussian was fitted to the psychometric function. 
[121] From this curve the magnitude of eye turn which was judged correctly on 84% of 
[122] trials was estimated, corresponding to the standard deviation of the observer’s internal 
[123] error distribution. We will call this threshold value the minimum detectable eye turn.

[131] (d) Results 
[132] The graphs in Figure 2 show minimum detectable eye turn plotted as a function of 
[133] viewing distance. Each graph shows data for one observer at one stimulus duration. 
[134] The two lines on each graph are for the two eyebrow conditions: normal and lowered. 
[135] For both observers and in all conditions the data show one striking feature: sensitivity 
[136] to gaze deviation is unaffected by viewing distance, up to a certain critical distance. 
[137] Beyond this distance, performance gets rapidly worse. These curves could be 
[138] characterized by two parameters: the optimal sensitivity and the critical distance. Both 
[139] of these parameters are affected by stimulus duration: reducing stimulus duration from 
[140] 1000 ms to 20 ms increases the minimum detectable eye turn from about 0.8° to about 
[141] 1.3°, and causes a decrease in the critical distance from about 15 m to about 8 m. 
[142] Lowering the eyebrows does not affect the optimal sensitivity, but it does appear to 
[143] change the critical viewing distance for short duration stimuli.
