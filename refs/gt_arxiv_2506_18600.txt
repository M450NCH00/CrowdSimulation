                                                                                                  Abstract

                                                    A potential concern when simulating populations of large language models (LLMs)
                                                is data contamination, i.e. the possibility that training data may shape outcomes in
                                                unintended ways. While this concern is important and may hinder certain experiments
                                                with multi-agent models, it does not preclude the study of genuinely emergent dynamics
                                                in LLM populations. The recent critique by Barrie and T¨ornberg [1] of the results of
                                                Flint Ashery et al. [2] offers an opportunity to clarify that self-organisation and model-
                                                dependent emergent dynamics can be studied in LLM populations, highlighting how
                                                such dynamics have been empirically observed in the specific case of social conventions.

                                            Barrie & To¨rnberg [1] question whether the emergence of conventions observed in our
                                        recent study of interacting large language models (LLMs) [2] can be attributed to genuine
                                        collective dynamics, or instead result from data leakage from the models' training data. In
                                        this note, we respond to their main points and argue that the observed dynamics cannot
                                        be explained by data contamination alone. We also note that our study reported two
                                        additional results - emergent collective bias and critical mass dynamics - that are likewise
                                        the outcome of self-organising population dynamics. We hope this response will contribute
                                        to ongoing methodological discussions in this area of research, at a time when collective
                                        AI dynamics are quickly gaining societal relevance [3].

                                            Specifically, Barrie & To¨rnberg [1] raise two main critiques of [2]. The first concerns
                                        inventory pruning: the authors argue that the inventory update rule in our naming game
                                        (agents retaining the last matched convention) makes the emergence of consensus trivial.
                                        The second, more central, critique, reflected in the title of their contribution, concerns data
                                        leakage: the authors suggest that LLMs may recognise the scenario (as a coordination game
                                        or naming game) from training data, and may therefore be reproducing known outcomes

points separately below.

    Before proceeding, it is important to highlight how the words "emergence" and "con-
vention" are used in [2]. Following the standard approach in complex and social systems
[4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10], we use the term "emergence" when "purely local interactions [..]
lead to population-wide, or `global,' coordinated behavior" "without the intervention of any
centralized institution"1 and we use "convention" to refer to a pattern of behavior shared
throughout a community, defined as the outcome that everyone expects in interactions
allowing two or more equivalent actions [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. A convention is therefore
emergent when it "arise[s] spontaneously, without the intervention of any centralized insti-
tution," i.e., when "agents are endowed with purely local incentives and conventions may
(or may not) emerge as an unintended consequence of individuals attempting to coordinate
locally with one another."2[2].

Inventory Pruning

The naming game framework adopted in [2] is a standard experimental paradigm for study-
ing convention formation in both artificial [21, 22, 23] and human [24, 25] populations. The
inventory update rule is an integral part of this paradigm and is implemented accordingly.
Our goal was not to test whether conventions emerge under arbitrary conditions, but to
study how LLM populations behave in a well-established experimental setting for conven-
tion formation, allowing us to characterise their collective dynamics and compare them,
where appropriate, with human experiments and theoretical models. As stated in [22],
which introduced the standard theoretical model, "We want to understand how the mi-
croscopic dynamics of the agent interactions can nevertheless give rise to global coherence
without external intervention." In this light, the focus is not on how a pair of individuals
reaches consensus, but on how different pairs, each reaching local consensus on potentially
different conventions, can ultimately give rise to global consensus.

    In this sense, the objection in [1] that "That the LLM `picks' this word in subsequent
rounds is therefore trivial" overlooks an important aspect of the dynamics. In a setting
where subsequent interactions of a successful pair involve other agents who are new to
them, strong local convergence may complicate global consensus, as persisting with the

    It is also important to stress that the findings in [2] go beyond demonstrating conver-
gence. We observed emergent collective bias: conventions were selected collectively with
non-uniform probabilities, even when individual agents were initially unbiased. We also
observed critical mass tipping points: small committed minorities were able to overturn
established conventions, matching predictions from human experiments and theoretical
models. These phenomena too cannot be explained by inventory pruning alone, which
operates locally and symmetrically.

Data Leakage and Prior Knowledge

We explicitly acknowledge the risk of data contamination in [2], where we contrast AI-
specific and human-like collective dynamics, and highlight that some behaviours (e.g. col-
lective bias) appear to be LLM-specific phenomena, meriting further study. In the Supple-
mentary Text ("Prompt Bias Mitigation"), we note that: "the game's population dynamics
and the symmetry in payoffs across action labels ensure that no predetermined optimal strat-
egy exists--even if a player were aware of global information. Convergence occurs purely
at the local level, driven by repeated random interactions rather than a global rule that
dictates strategic behaviour." In other words, even if an LLM recognises the game struc-
ture, the observed population-level dynamics (symmetry breaking, collective bias, critical
mass) depend on history-dependent, decentralised interactions, not on a globally optimal
solution.

    In fact, our choice of the coordination game setting was deliberate, precisely in light
of the potential for data leakage. In contrast to games with a clearly defined optimal
strategy (such as many classical game-theoretical setups) or a well-established non-rational
outcome (such as the ultimatum game3), coordination games with equivalent alternatives,
such as the naming game, do not specify which convention or action should emerge. This
significantly limits the effects of agents' prior knowledge, making these games especially
suitable for studying whether and how global consensus can arise from decentralised local
interactions.

    Returning to the line of reasoning in [1], the argument appears to be that: (i) LLMs
recognise that they are playing a coordination game; (ii) they know from training that
repeating the same choice is a good, or optimal, strategy; and (iii) their training data
suggests that such games should converge, so they do. There are several reasons why this
explanation does not account for our results.

    The starting point for this reasoning is [1]'s meta-prompting. Three meta-prompting
questions are asked to the LLM after providing it with the prompt designed in [2]. They

   1. "Does this setup remind you of any existing model or theory in social science?"

    To answer the first question, the LLM recognises a coordination game, adding that it is
a "repeated version of such a game". In response to the second question, the LLM replies:
"After a `success' [..], the optimal move is to repeat the same action in the next round."
Finally, in response to the third question, the LLM summarises its output as: "Rapid
convergence to a single, repeated action (a convention), maximizing joint and individual
payoffs." [1]. These answers reveal two key aspects.

    The first aspect is that the LLM interprets "globally" as "pairwise", not as referring
to population-level interactions (see:"Early rounds might involve mismatches as players
`search' for a common choice, but once they successfully coordinate, both have a strong
incentive to continue repeating that choice." [1] [underlining our own]). Interestingly, this
pattern holds across several models (see Appendix). We also conducted further experi-
ments, following the meta-prompting approach of [1], with a final question: "Finally, do
you think that you are playing against a single player or a whole population?". The re-
sults confirm that all tested models assume they are playing against a single opponent
(see Appendix). Naturally, assuming pairwise interactions is perfectly consistent with our
experimental setup: the prompt explicitly introduces a two-player interaction and, as we
note, it "does not specify that agents are part of a population or provide any detail on how
the interaction partner is selected from a group"[2].

    The second aspect of the LLM's answer to the meta-prompting worth noting is that,
as a consequence of this pairwise framing, the LLM concludes that repeating the same
name after a success is "optimal", and that "rational players will `lock in' to the first
successful coordination point and maintain it for the remainder of the rounds." However, if
this were the case (i.e., if agents "lock[ed] themselves in to the first successful coordination"
name) this behaviour would not be optimal in a population setting. On the contrary, it
would hinder the emergence of global consensus, since different pairs initially coordinate
on different names. In other words, the locally optimal strategy would actively prevent
global consensus from forming.

    Thus, firstly, the LLM interpretation of the experiment as involving pairwise inter-
actions, consistent with the prompt and experimental design, strongly suggests that the
LLMs are not relying on prior information in this setting, since most, if not all, papers
on the naming game, and a significant portion of the literature on coordination games,
involve large populations [26, 27, 9]. Secondly, the meta-prompting in [1] does not explain
the global consensus observed in our experiments. In fact, it supports the view that LLMs
focus on local coordination while neglecting global dynamics, in line with the experimental

design in [2], but they do not rigidly adopt the locally optimal strategy they return when
meta-prompted (or we would not observe global convergence). This further reinforces the
validity of our experimental approach and the emergent nature of consensus on a shared
social convention.
A number of other points are relevant:

    First, even if the LLM agents inferred from prior knowledge that privileging the same
symbol after a coordination success is a reasonable strategy, this would not be problematic:
it is a natural inference given our prompt, which by design incentivises local convergence
and does not specify the population size, or even whether there is a population at all. To
converge, however, agents must be able to adapt their choices to the evolving ecology of
conventions they encounter as the experiment unfolds. In lab experiments with human
participants, several users reported that they guessed they were no longer playing with the
same partner at a certain point during the game [24]. Further investigation into how LLMs
update their perception of the game as it unfolds would be a valuable direction for future
work.

    Second, considering also the aspect of collective bias, if the model were somehow sim-
ulating a pre-scripted trajectory towards a predetermined convention, the committed mi-
nority experiments reported in [2] would fail. The LLM would ignore the actual game
context and converge, or maintain convergence, on the predetermined symbol. In fact, the
success of the committed minority experiments demonstrates that the model behaviour is
context-sensitive and dynamically influenced by interactions. Furthermore, if the LLMs
were simply recognising the game and executing an optimal strategy (as [1] suggests), such
minority-driven reversals should not occur since, once population consensus is reached,
maintaining the winning convention is precisely the standard "optimal" behaviour in co-
ordination games, and the LLMs know this, as noted above.

    Third, a specific result appears to directly challenge the data leakage argument. In
the Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct populations, we observed cases where the population spon-
taneously loses consensus and switches to a different "stronger" convention without any
committed agents (Figure 3B, and text: "In Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct populations, the bias
is so strongly weighted against the weaker convention that the population spontaneously
switches to the alternative, stronger convention without requiring any committed agents at
all." See also Figure S7 of [2] for the production probability over time). Moreover, this
behaviour, stemming from the collective bias phenomenon, is model-dependent: it is ob-
served in Llama-3.1 but not uniformly across models. All of this supports the conclusion
that population-level dynamics in LLMs emerge from complex, model-specific interaction
effects.

    Fourth, broadening the scope beyond the naming game experiments, the fact that a
model recognises a specific game does not prove that the model will not interpret the prompt
literally, without incorporating additional assumptions from training data. In other words,
the prompt itself could override or constrain prior knowledge, and the test proposed in [1]
does not establish that this is not the case. This is an important general point, although

                                                          5
it is difficult to test conclusively.
    Finally, again looking beyond our specific experiments, there is broader evidence that

the data leakage explanation does not fully account for LLM behaviour in related domains.
In the growing literature on LLMs in cooperation games, there are several cases where
LLM behaviour diverges from what is documented in human experiments, or from what
might be expected from an agent that "knows the trick" of the game. For instance, in
experiments on the Prisoner's Dilemma [28], LLMs do not behave like typical humans,
rational agents, or fully game-aware agents who would simply cooperate throughout. These
observations further suggest that LLM population dynamics are not simply reproducing
patterns memorised from training data.

    Further insights from collective bias. A further point that is not fully addressed
in [1] concerns collective bias. [1] mentions bias only once, in the sentence: "They also
carry out a number of mitigation steps to guard against the potential for bias towards
one single token (i.e., some baked-in preference for a given character of letter meaning
any convergence is artefactual of model bias)." However, our mitigation strategy concerns
individual bias. A key finding of [2] is that even when individual agents are tested to
be unbiased in isolation, collective bias emerges as a population-level effect, driven by
the dynamics of interaction and memory accumulation. Crucially, this effect is model-
dependent, and the strength and direction of collective bias vary across LLM architectures,
as shown in our results. It is also non-deterministic (i.e., convergence does not happen
systematically on the same convention). Therefore, this phenomenon reflects the emergent
dynamics of the interacting system and is hard to reduce to data leakage or static biases
in training data alone.

Conclusion

We agree that data contamination is an important methodological challenge in this line of
work, and we welcome continued discussion on best practices. Our paper explicitly flags
this as a potential limitation and highlights the need for further study. However, for all
the reasons outlined above, the core contributions of our work remain valid and are not
explained by the concerns raised. Of course, more work is needed, and we already have
preliminary results that further clarify how "more is different" [4] also for populations
of LLMs [29]. We look forward to further constructive exchanges on these important
questions.

The example response provided in the main text of Ref. [1] suggests that the game takes
place between two players. Further inspection of [1]'s data repository confirms that this
is not unique: across different models, the majority of responses similarly used the term
"both", or otherwise implied that the prompt described a two-player game. Following the
meta-prompting approach conducted by Barrie & To¨rnberg [1], we also tested whether the
LLM infers that it is engaged in a social experiment with a population, or instead interprets
the experiment as involving only two players. We used the same prompt template as in
[1], replacing the final question about global convergence with the following: "Finally, do
you think that you are playing against a single player or a whole population?" This meta-
prompting test was repeated five times for each tested model4. Every response across all
tested models indicated that the experiment involves two players, rather than a population.
However, the most capable LLMs also correctly noted that if the experiment were conducted
in populations, both the optimal strategy and the overall convergence dynamics would
depend on the evolution of the system. They did not, however, interpret this as the
setup described in the prompt (see GPT-4.1 example reported below). In this regard,
it is important to stress that in the experiments of [2], and in the naming game more
broadly, agents are never presented with information about "the frequency of choices in
the population" (as mentioned in the GPT-4.1 example) or any other metrics describing
collective behaviour.
