    8| Abstract.Genuine spatial reasoning relies on the capacity to con-
    9| struct and manipulate coherent internal spatial representations, of-
   10| ten conceptualized as mental models, rather than merely processing
   11| surface linguistic associations. While large language models exhibit
   12| advanced capabilities across various domains, existing benchmarks
   13| fail to isolate this intrinsic spatial cognition from statistical language
   14| heuristics. Furthermore, multimodal evaluations frequently conflate
   15| genuine spatial reasoning with visual perception. To systematically
   16| investigate whether models construct flexible spatial mental mod-
   17| els, we introduce SpatialText, a theory-driven diagnostic framework.
   18| Rather than functioning simply as a dataset, SpatialText isolates text-
   19| based spatial reasoning through a dual-source methodology. It in-
   20| tegrates human-annotated descriptions of real 3D indoor environ-
   21| ments—which capture natural ambiguities, perspective shifts, and
   22| functional relations—with code-generated, logically precise scenes
   23| designed to probe formal spatial deduction and epistemic boundaries.
   24| Systematic evaluation across state-of-the-art models reveals funda-
   25| mental representational limitations. Although models demonstrate
   26| proficiency in retrieving explicit spatial facts and operating within
   27| global, allocentric coordinate systems, they exhibit critical failures
   28| in egocentric perspective transformation and local reference frame
   29| reasoning. These systematic errors provide strong evidence that cur-
   30| rent models rely heavily on linguistic co-occurrence heuristics rather
   31| than constructing coherent, verifiable internal spatial representations.
   32| SpatialText thus serves as a rigorous instrument for diagnosing the
   33| cognitive boundaries of artificial spatial intelligence.

  196| Annotation StrategyTo probe different dimensions of tex-
  197| tual spatial reasoning, annotations are designed under three dis-
  198| tinct reference-frame strategies: egocentric, allocentric, and hybrid.
  199| Rather than merely describing object locations, these strategies sys-
  200| tematically manipulate how spatial relations are framed, thereby im-
  201| posing different cognitive demands on the model.
  202| Egocentric descriptions encode relations relative to an observer or
  203| the intrinsic orientation of an object (e.g., “to the left of the bed”).
  204| This strategy emphasizes local spatial reasoning and object-centered
  205| perspective alignment.
  206| Allocentric descriptions rely on environment-centered anchors,
  207| such as cardinal directions or fixed global references. This requires
  208| maintaining a stable global spatial representation independent of ob-
  209| server position.
  210| Hybrid descriptions combine absolute orientation cues with rela-
  211| tive or clock-based expressions (e.g., “at the 3 o’clock direction on
  212| the east side”). This formulation introduces cross-reference-frame
  213| transformation, requiring the model to dynamically reconcile mul-
  214| tiple coordinate systems within a single internal representation.
