                                            In a recent contribution, Ashery et al. [1] argue that interacting large lan-
                                        guage models (LLMs) spontaneously exhibit emergent norms characteristic of
                                        simulated models of human populations. Specifically, they instantiate a ver-
                                        sion of the classic "naming game" wherein two agents are randomly paired and
                                        can choose from a lexicon of potential conventions/words. When two agents
                                        choose the same word, they receive a reward; when they choose different words,
                                        they receive a penalty. When the agents match, they pare down their lexicon
                                        to the last matched word, mechanically tilting the balance in favor of future
                                        matches on that convention/word. The key contribution of this model is that
                                        it demonstrates that decentralized agents represented through simple rules can
                                        spontaneously converge on a shared linguistic convention through local pairwise
                                        interactions, without central coordination.

                                            In their version, Ashery et al. do not represent the agents through a set
                                        of simple rules, but through zero-shot LLMs. More specifically, they prompt a
                                        platform-based or locally stored LLM with the following prompt:

    They demonstrate that this prompt, as well as its variations, leads to the
global emergence of social conventions after a certain number of game rounds.
They use various different LLMs (Llama 2, Llama 3.0, Llama 3.1, Claude 3.5)
and show that this global convergence dynamic is common to all models. They
also carry out a number of mitigation steps to guard against the potential for
bias towards one single token (i.e., some baked-in preference for a given character
of letter meaning any convergence is artefactual of model bias). They introduce
both bulleted and narrative prompts to determine the consistency of models
across prompt templates and find that the results hold. They use nonsensical
strings as the inventory of words as well as numeric formulations. Finally, they
use meta prompting strategies to ensure the models are correctly understanding
the payoffs they have received and their history of choices.

    But what the authors claim to be the "spontaneous" emergence of social
conventions among a population of LLMs is likely to be something far more
prosaic. The inventory pruning rule is hard-coded into their simulation code
at https://github.com/cjbarrie/AI-norms/blob/e57aeef151a46108fd
1521c66e345e557e57dbf6/NG_module.py#L75. As such, it is by mechanics
statistically more likely to get a success on this word/convention in subsequent
pairings. That the LLM "picks" this word in subsequent rounds is therefore
trivial.

    There is however a second problem, with implications for the broader gen-
erative ABM literature: the LLM may already know what answer to give, as a
result of "data leakage" or "contamination" [2].1 Data leakage ­ that is, that
the model is tested or evaluated on data on which it was previously trained ­
represents a well-known but often manageable challenge when using LLMs for

data analysis. However, in relation to generative ABMs, it represents a simulta-
neously more fundamental and less recognized challenge: the existing scientific
literature is likely to be part of the training data of the LLM, which means that
the it may simply be reproducing its knowledge of previous research findings.
In the case of the paper by Ashery et al., this would mean that the individual
agents recognize the scenario as a "naming game" or some form of coordination
on convention setup. If the LLM does recognize this, it would mean it knows
how the local choices it makes can lead to global convergence on a given norm.
Rather than offering a novel research finding, the model may hence be simply
regurgitating the results of existing research findings from its training data.

    None of the mitigation steps taken by the authors guards sufficiently against
this risk. The authors try to address this concern in the Supplementary Material
to [1] where they note:

       "In considering whether bias in the prompt arises from the pres-
       ence of the experimental framework in the training data, we note
       that the game's population dynamics and the symmetry in payoffs
       across action labels ensure that no predetermined optimal strategy
       exists--even if a player were aware of global information. Conver-
       gence occurs purely at the local level, driven by repeated random
       interactions rather than a global rule that dictates strategic behav-
       ior."

    But what Ashery et al. claim in their contribution to be "emergent social
conventions" are observationally equivalent to a series of LLM agents mapping
the payoff description to its pretraining knowledge of coordination games. That
is, these agents could be behaving like human agents in the real world or they
could simply be reproducing what they know agents tend to do in the context
of these games.

    Fortunately, we can easily determine whether or not the LLM is aware of the
kind of scenario we are placing it in: we can ask it. Using the same prompt as
the authors used in the original paper, we prompt a series of LLMs as follows:2

    For each LLM, we ask the model for its output ten times. It is clear from
these experiments that the model knows both what type of game it is as well as
likely optimal moves after success and ultimate convergence. Here is an example
response when querying the OpenAI model gpt-4.1:

       1. Analogy to Existing Model/Theory in Social Science:

       This setup closely resembles the Coordination Game in game
       theory|a classic model in economics and social science. In
       a coordination game, players benefit from making the same
       choices, and are penalized for failing to coordinate. The
       most famous example is the ``Battle of the Sexes,'' but your
       version is a pure coordination game with symmetric payoffs
       for matching and a symmetric penalty for mismatching.

       Additionally, this setup is a repeated version of such a game,
       which is often studied in the context of Repeated Games and
       Social Convention Formation. Over time, players can learn
       to coordinate on a convention (e.g., always picking the same
       number), an analogy to how social conventions or norms emerge
       in societies.

       2. Optimal Move After a Success:

       After a ``success'' (i.e., both players chose the same action
       and got +100), the optimal move is to repeat the same action
       in the next round. This maximizes expected payoff, since
       any deviation risks moving from +100 to {50. In repeated
       coordination games, this is known as ``sticking with the established
       convention.''

       3. Global Convergence of the Game:

       Globally, the game will almost always converge to persistent
       coordination on a single action. Early rounds might involve

       mismatches as players ``search'' for a common choice, but
       once they successfully coordinate, both have a strong incentive
       to continue repeating that choice. Any deviation is heavily
       punished, so rational players will ``lock in'' to the first
       successful coordination point and maintain it for the remainder
       of the rounds.

       In summary:

           · Analogy: Coordination game / social convention formation.

           · Optimal move after success: Repeat the same action.

           · Global convergence: Rapid convergence to a single, repeated
              action (a convention), maximizing joint and individual
              payoffs.

    Put simply, while the model does not explicitly identify this as a "naming
game" setup, it does understand the basic structure of the scenario as well as
optimal moves after success and what global convergence will look like.

    We conducted this analysis across a range of different LLMs. We then also
used the OpenAI model gpt-4.1 to annotate three dimensions of the different
LLM model outputs: whether it identified the setup as a coordination game;
whether it correctly identified the optimal move; and whether it was able to
correctly predict how the scenario would converge globally. We also asked the
model to output the text snippet from the model output of the given LLM that
the OpenAI model used to justify its decision.3 We then manually verify all of
these annotations on the basis of the text snippet provided. We plot the results
in Figure 1. We see that for most models, including those used in the original
paper, the models were able to correctly identify each of these dimensions. They
are less able to predict the global convergence of the model--but many of the
models manage this too.

    As a result, what we observe in the interaction of LLM agents may not be
the "spontaneous" development of conventions as argued by the authors but the
more prosaic mirroring of model training data. This is a problem that befalls
not just the contribution by Ashery et al. but arguably all simulations that rely
on LLMs reproducing "known" emergent properties of complex human systems.
Simply put, the emergent behaviors we believe we are seeing could just be the
model reflecting its training data back at us.

    So what would we need to see to believe that the LLMs were, in fact, self-
organizing in ways similar to human populations? Modifying the model to hide
the type of model through obfuscation ­ say, for instance, by using unicode
characters as tokens ­ is unlikely to prevent the LLM from recognizing the
general structure of the game with sufficient certainty. One solution would be
to "invent" some completely novel game that the LLM has definitely not seen

be a fundamental and intractable issue without strict control over the training

    While there is an emerging literature on strategies for combating data leak-
age, this field is far from settled [5]. Some promising avenues include inter-
pretability probing with sparse autoencoders [4] as well as the measurement of
next-token perplexity based on a given prompt setup [7]. Whether or not the
application of these techniques to generative ABM settings would help overcome
some of the problems we describe above is an open question, however.

    Overall, it remains a steep challenge to make claims of emergent behav-
ior using existing off-the-shelf LLMs, in particular when using the models to
reproduce or engage with existing research results. While conducting social
simulations using LLMs remains a promising agenda, we believe the question of
data contamination should be at the heart of future research in this area.

    The question of data leakage in generative ABMs however also raises deeper
philosophical questions. A long research tradition has shown that humans,
too, are affected by research results. Most relevantly to the study at hand,
research has found that economics students are more likely to reach equilibrium
outcomes in coordination games [3]--a human form of "data leakage". Anthony
Giddens [6] famously described this as a fundamental condition of the social
sciences: unlike in the natural sciences, social science concepts can "enter into"

shaped by the very act of scientific description, raising deep questions about
their role in social scientific models.
