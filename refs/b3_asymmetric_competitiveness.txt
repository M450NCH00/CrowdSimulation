Abstract: In order to investigate the influence of factors such as pheromones and avoidance behavior on the evacuation of heterogeneous crowds, a multi-speed cellular automata evacuation model based on asymmetric competitiveness is proposed for the evacuation of the complex groups in a single-exit room. By optimizing the crowd density and pedestrian speed equations, multi-speed heterogeneous crowds can be obtained in the model. In order to achieve the description of a multi-dimensional asymmetric competitiveness heterogeneous population, the evacuation competitiveness is considered in the pedestrians with different speed, age, gender, etc., and by considering the avoidance character existing among pedestrians, the avoidance behavior is also discussed in this model. It is well known that the information received by different pedestrians is different. In order to consider the asymmetry of information, the pheromones are introduced into the evacuation model to discuss the effect of information differences on evacuation. The evacuation results show that the asymmetry of information has a facilitating effect on the evacuation speed of pedestrians, and the best evacuation effect is obtained when the radius of the pheromone is about 3 m. Moreover, evacuation time is weakly correlated with pedestrians' gender but strongly correlated with pedestrians' age. The avoidance behavior plays a positive role in evacuation, and the evacuation time reaches the minimum value when the avoidance probability is about 0.5. The slope of the reduction in evacuation time is greatest when the avoidance threshold is 0.4 to 0.8. The findings can support evacuation capacity assessment, emergency planning, and decision making.
Keywords: pheromones; heterogeneous crowds; avoidance behavior; cellular automata; personnel evacuation
1. Introduction
With the acceleration of urbanization, there has been a significant increase in high-rise buildings, large-scale public facilities, and entertainment venues in urban areas. It is well known that most of those places are densely populated; thus, the evacuations in those places are difficult and time-consuming when some disasters, such as fires, earthquakes, or man-made accidents, happen. In the event of a disaster, it is undoubtedly of great significance to reduce the loss of life and property if a more reasonable evacuation strategy can be adopted [1­3]. Fortunately, in recent years, based on the efforts made by scholars, some results about pedestrians' evacuation were also achieved. For example, literature [4] achieved customized evacuation guidance services for different populations by integrating

the effects of gender, age, and psychological state on individual evacuation behavior. Literature [5] suggested that the rational use of evacuation simulation tools can help predict the behavior and evacuation time of evacuees under different environmental factors. By introducing the symmetry-breaking effect of pedestrian behavior, literature [6] showed some improvement in the accuracy of the model in simulating pedestrian behavior. According to the model scales, the existing evacuation models can be categorized into three types: macro, micro, and meso evacuation models [7­9]. The cellular automata (CA) model, as a kind of macro model, has some significant advantages, such as high efficiency, scalability, and simplicity of implementation, and it has attracted extensive attention from scholars in recent years, e.g., ship evacuation models considering pedestrian interaction [10], subway station evacuation models considering guidance [11], and models dealing with large-scale crowd evacuation [12]. At the same time, Kirchner et al. [13] proposed a method for simulating dynamic fields by diffusion and attenuation that mimics chemotaxis to describe pedestrian interactions. Some scholars simulated the movement behavior of the pedestrians in fire accident by introducing the pheromone-like dynamic fields [14,15]. Burstedde et al. [16] proposed an improved evacuation algorithm by applying asymmetric pheromone dynamic fields.
The related studies provided some references for modeling evacuation, obviously. However, it is worth pointing out that using single personnel attribute to describe the evacuees is unreasonable because most of the evacuees are complex. For example, the evacuees can be composed of the individuals of different ages, genders, and physical qualities, and different kinds of individuals have different evacuation performances, obviously. Thus, considering the asymmetry of the people's movement characteristics is necessary and important. Fortunately, scholars have made some efforts in this area, and some results have been achieved. For example, Fang et al. [17] found that women have some disadvantages over men during evacuation by performing some virtual reality experiments. Hu et al. [18] studied the evacuation characteristics of partially differing crowds based on a cellular automata model by introducing asymmetric competitiveness to obtain a power function jointly determined by the external environment and pedestrian perception. Qiu et al. [19] developed an extended CA model considering competitiveness to deal with the simultaneous evacuation of pedestrians with multiple speeds in the model. The above related studies, while shedding light on the heterogeneity of personnel competitiveness, failed to extend sufficiently to other dimensions of heterogeneity in competitiveness across sport types and pedestrians. In order to consider more broadly the heterogeneity of different movement types and pedestrian competitiveness, Wang et al. [20] argued that pedestrians' behavior in emergencies is influenced by a variety of factors, such as age, gender, and familiarity with the environment. Forrest et al. [21] found significant correlations between gender and age and speed and stride frequency of individuals. The statistics of Shi et al. [22] showed that males were overall 0.2 m/s to 0.3 m/s faster than females on average. Meanwhile, some scholars have concluded that the walking speed of a human is 0.8~1.8 m/s [23], the average evacuation speed is 2.03 m/s in a smoke-free situation (the average age is 41.3 years old) [24], and the evacuation speed is 1.2~2.19 m/s in emergency situations [25] through field tests or data analysis. Predtechenskii et al. analyzed the trend of evacuation velocity under normal and fire conditions in literature [26]. Togawa et al. derived the relationship between evacuation velocity and crowd density in literature [27]. Fang et al. [28] dealt with the evacuation response of people by using kinetic flow principles, while the empirical evacuation response equations were further refined.
The above related results provide a good generalization and analysis of evacuation velocity variability but less involved in evacuation and physical simulation of complex groups with multiple velocities. To address the above problems, in order to understand

4. Conclusions
This paper focuses on the complex crowd evacuation of single-exit rooms in emergency situations and proposes a multi-speed cellular automata evacuation model based on asymmetric competitiveness. The model realizes the dynamic adjustment of pedestrian speed and describes the multidimensional competitiveness of heterogeneous crowds. The effects of competition-derived behaviors (avoidance behaviors) and pheromones on the evacuation of heterogeneous crowds are explored. The research results show that: (1) A crowd density of 0.375 persons/m2 is the watershed for the rate of increase in
evacuation time. At crowd densities greater than 0.375 persons/m2, the risk of evacuation increases, and a pheromone dispersion radius of 2 to 4 m is optimal. The increase in personnel density and pheromone diffusion radius leads to a decrease in the gradient of pheromone distribution, and when there is no gradient difference in the pheromone received from the center, it becomes difficult for evacuees to choose a path, resulting in an increase in evacuation time. However, when the crowd density is less than 0.375 people/m2, a longer pheromone spreading radius can help pedestrians find the exit faster and shorten the evacuation time. Therefore, the role of pheromones is asymmetric, and the right amount of personnel density and pheromone radius can effectively help evacuees find the exit faster, thus, increasing the evacuation speed; (2) When the number of evacuees is small, the effect of gender on evacuation is small. When the number of evacuees is large, the effect of gender on evacuation is larger, and under the same total number of people, the larger the proportion of men, the shorter the evacuation time. Gender has less influence on the evacuation time of the elderly, and changes in the ratio of men to women do not significantly affect their arrival time at the exit. Elderly people are a vulnerable group in the evacuation process, and the gap between their evacuation ability and that of young and middle-aged people is obvious, and they are easily crowded by young and middle-aged people who cannot move inside the crowd or are at the end of the crowd due to their slow moving speed; (3) The avoidance behavior was effective in reducing the evacuation time, and the intense competition for grid points near the exits was significantly reduced. When young and middle-aged people are the main body of pedestrians, avoidance behavior has a greater effect of shortening evacuation time when the composition of pedestrians is complex. When older adults are pedestrian subjects, evacuation times are most

affected by avoidance behaviors. The above reflects the asymmetry of avoidance behavior. Evacuation time reaches a minimum at avoidance probabilities of 0.5 and decreases at a maximum rate at avoidance thresholds of 0.4 to 0.8. In practice, the competitiveness of the elderly can be considered as an avoidance threshold.
The findings can support evacuation capacity assessment, emergency planning, and decision making. However, the evacuation environment in this paper is relatively simple, and more evacuation factors will be incorporated in future studies, such as introducing environmental hazards (fire, smoke, obstacles, etc.) and considering group evacuation behavior (leader­follower dynamics, etc.).
